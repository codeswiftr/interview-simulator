## Post 1: The Anxiety Confession
**Source:** Blog Post 1 - Why Your Interview Anxiety Isn't Actually About the Interview

I bombed my Google interview in 2019.

Not because I didn't know the answer. 
Because I knew it—and couldn't access it.

My hands were shaking. My mind went blank. I rambled for 3 minutes about something completely unrelated.

The feedback: "Struggled to articulate technical concepts."

I'd been articulating technical concepts for 8 years. That wasn't the problem. 

The problem was anxiety.

Here's what I learned after 50+ more interviews:

Interview anxiety isn't a preparation problem. 
It's an exposure problem.

Your nervous system thinks you're in danger. 
More studying doesn't fix that. 
More practice under pressure does.

Every interview after that Google rejection, I cared a little less. 
Because I'd already survived the worst.

The practical takeaway:
→ Seek more practice under realistic conditions
→ Care about the reps, not the results
→ Let your nervous system learn that interviews aren't fatal

Your anxiety is lying to you. 
You probably know more than you think.

## Post 2: The STAR Method Problem
**Source:** Blog Post 2 - STAR Method Is Broken. Here's What Works Instead.

Unpopular opinion: The STAR method is making your interviews worse.

Every prep resource teaches it. 
Every candidate uses it. 
And most behavioral answers sound exactly the same.

Here's the issue:

STAR tells you to start with "Situation"—2-3 sentences of context.

But your interviewer doesn't care about context. 
They care about what YOU did.

When you lead with situation, you're burying the interesting part.

Try this instead:

Lead with conflict. 

Start in the middle, where things got hard.

"Everyone said the migration was impossible."
"Two days before launch, we found a critical bug."
"The customer was about to churn."

THEN give minimal context. 
THEN explain what you did.

The difference:

STAR: "The situation was our team was behind schedule..."
Conflict-first: "We had 48 hours and needed 2 weeks of work."

Same story. One sounds like a report. One sounds like a story worth hearing.

Your interviewer hears dozens of STAR answers. 
Be the one they remember.

## Post 3: The 90-Second Rule
**Source:** Blog Post 4 - How to Tell a Story That Gets You Hired

Behavioral interview answers should be 90-120 seconds.

Not 5 minutes. 
Not 30 seconds.

Here's why:

Under 60 seconds: You're not giving enough detail. It sounds like you're hiding something.

Over 2 minutes: You're losing them. Attention drops. Details blur together.

The structure:

0-10 seconds: Set up the conflict
10-70 seconds: What YOU did (specific, with reasoning)
70-90 seconds: Result + what you learned

That's it. 

Practice with a timer. 
Record yourself. 
Be ruthless about cutting.

The best interview answers aren't comprehensive. 
They're memorable.

## Post 4: The Senior Interview Trap
**Source:** Blog Post 5 - System Design Interviews: What Senior Engineers Actually Need to Know

The biggest mistake senior engineers make in interviews:

Designing immediately.

"Design a URL shortener."
*Starts drawing boxes*

This works at L4. It fails at Staff.

Why?

At senior levels, they're not testing if you CAN design. 
They're testing if you know WHICH design fits THESE constraints.

The fix:

Spend the first 5 minutes ONLY asking questions.

"What's the scale?"
"What are we optimizing for?"
"What constraints exist?"

This isn't stalling. 
It's demonstrating exactly what senior engineers get paid to do:
→ Understanding the problem before jumping to solutions

The design is the easy part. 
Defining what "good" means is the hard part.

Show them you know the difference.

## Post 6: The 2-Week Plan
**Source:** Blog Post 7 - The 2-Week Interview Prep Plan for Working Engineers

Interview in 2 weeks? Here's the plan:

Week 1:
- Day 1-2: Write + practice 5 behavioral stories (2h/day)
- Day 3-4: Master 5 core coding patterns (2h/day)
- Day 5: Company research (1h)
- Weekend: System design basics (4h)

Week 2:
- Day 8-9: Mock interviews (2h/day)
- Day 10: Address gaps from mocks (2h)
- Day 11-12: Polish and refine (1.5h/day)
- Weekend: Final mock + logistics

Interview day: Light exercise, good meal, no new studying.

Total time: 20 hours

The key:
Behavioral prep has the highest ROI. 
It's where most engineers are weakest. 
And it's faster to improve.

Good luck.

## Post 7: The Virtual Interview Setup
**Source:** Blog Post 8 - Virtual Interview Mistakes That Cost You the Offer

Your Zoom interview setup is sabotaging you.

Quick checklist:

□ Camera at eye level (not laptop looking up your nose)
□ Light source IN FRONT of you (not behind)
□ Look at camera lens when speaking (not the screen)
□ Headphones (reduces echo, looks professional)
□ Neutral background (or blur it)

The "energy" problem:

What feels normal in person reads as flat on video.

Increase your energy 20%. 
Speak slightly louder. 
Use more facial expressions.

It'll feel over-the-top to you. 
It'll look natural to them.

Virtual interviews have different rules. 
Master the medium.

## Post 8: Why You're Failing Behavioral
**Source:** Blog Post 9 - Why You're Failing Behavioral Interviews (And How to Fix It)

You're failing behavioral interviews for one of these reasons:

1. Too polished
Your stories sound rehearsed. Include moments of uncertainty. Show you're human.

2. Wrong question
"Tell me about a conflict" doesn't mean "technical disagreement." Listen to the actual question.

3. Too vague
"Improved performance significantly" means nothing. "Reduced latency from 400ms to 80ms" means everything.

4. No failures
Everyone fails. If you never share struggles, you seem either dishonest or unaware.

5. No reasoning
"Why that approach?" "It seemed right." ← This kills you at senior levels.

The pattern:
Interviewers want to understand how you think. 

When you're too perfect, they can't see you. 
When you're too vague, they don't know what you actually did.

Show your real thinking. That's what they're evaluating.

## Post 9: Negotiation Script
**Source:** Blog Post 10 - Salary Negotiation for Software Engineers: Scripts That Work

The exact words to say when negotiating salary:

"I'm really excited about this opportunity. Based on my experience with [specific skill] and my research on market rates, I was hoping we could get to [$X]. Is there flexibility there?"

What this does:
→ Shows enthusiasm (they need to feel you'll accept)
→ Provides justification (not just "I want more")
→ Leaves room for discussion (not an ultimatum)

What NOT to say:
"Can you do better?" (too vague)
"I need..." (sounds desperate)
"Another company offered..." (unless true)

The average engineer who negotiates gets 10-15% more. 

On a $150K base, that's $15-22K per year.

One conversation. That's the difference.

Every engineer I know has felt this before interviews.

Here's what I tell them:

The interviewer is not trying to expose you.

Your role:

You're not faking it.
