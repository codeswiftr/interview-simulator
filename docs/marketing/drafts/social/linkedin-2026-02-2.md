## Post 6: The Imposter Syndrome Pattern
**Target:** Sam (The Imposter)
**Hook:** "They're going to realize I don't belong."

**Body:**
Every engineer I know has felt this right before an interview. Even the Principal Engineers. Even the ones with 15 years of experience.

The thought loop is always the same:
"I got lucky with my last job."
"Everyone else here is smarter than me."
"If they ask me X, I'm doomed."

Here's the reframe that stops the spiral:

The interviewer is not trying to expose you.
They are trying to find reasons to **hire** you.

Think about it. Recruiting is expensive. Reviewing resumes is boring. They want you to be the one. They want to stop interviewing.

Your job isn't to "trick" them into thinking you're smart.
Your job is to remove the obstacles—anxiety, vague stories, bad audio—that hide the competence you already have.

You're not faking it. You're just learning to show it.

**CTA:**
When was the last time you felt like an imposter? Did the reality match the fear?

## Post 7: The Eye Contact Illusion
**Target:** All Personas (Remote Interviews)
**Hook:** You're making eye contact with your interviewer. But they think you're looking at your shoes.

**Body:**
In remote interviews, geometry works against you.

When you look at the interviewer's face on your screen, you are looking down or to the side relative to your camera.
To them, it looks like you're reading notes, checking Slack, or just disengaged.

**The Fix:**
Look at the camera lens when you speak.
Look at the screen when you listen.

It feels unnatural. You're talking to a plastic circle instead of a human face. But to the person on the other side, it looks like confident, direct eye contact.

**Two other remote quick-fixes:**
1. **Lighting:** Put a lamp *behind* your laptop, facing you. Backlighting turns you into a silhouette.
2. **Audio:** Wear headphones. Laptop mics pick up fan noise and echo.

In a virtual interview, your setup is your first impression. Don't let a bad camera angle cost you the job.

**CTA:**
Check your webcam position right now. Is it at eye level, or is it looking up your nose?

## Post 8: The 48-Hour Negotiation Rule
**Target:** All Personas (Salary Negotiation)
**Hook:** You got the offer. The hard part is over. (Actually, the expensive part just started.)

**Body:**
The biggest mistake engineers make isn't failing the coding round.
It's accepting the offer on the spot.

"That sounds great, I accept!"

That sentence can cost you $15k-$50k a year.

**The 48-Hour Rule:**
No matter how good the offer is, never accept immediately.

Say this instead:
"Thank you so much! I'm really excited about this opportunity. I'd like to take 48 hours to review the full details and discuss it with my family. Can we touch base on Thursday?"

**Why this works:**
1. It shows you're professional and thoughtful.
2. It gives you time to research market rates on Levels.fyi or Blind.
3. It creates a tiny bit of tension that reminds them they want you.

Then, when you call back, you have a specific number ready. "Based on market data..."

Negotiation isn't conflict. It's business. Treat it that way.

**CTA:**
What's the best advice you've ever received about negotiating salary?

## Post 9: Don't Recite Your Resume
**Target:** Jordan (Career Switchers) & Juniors
**Hook:** "Tell me about yourself." (Please don't read me your resume.)

**Body:**
I've sat through hundreds of interviews. 90% of candidates answer this question chronologically.

"I went to college in 2018, then I worked at Company A, then I learned Python, then I joined Company B..."

I have your resume. I can read the dates.
This answer is boring because it lacks a **narrative**.

Try the **Present-Past-Future** formula instead:

1. **Present (The Hook):** "I'm a backend engineer who specializes in high-scale API design. Currently, I'm building..."
2. **Past (The Proof):** "Before that, I spent 5 years in [Previous Field], where I learned to handle complex stakeholder management—a skill that helps me deeply understand product requirements now."
3. **Future (The Fit):** "I'm looking for a role where I can tackle distributed systems challenges, which is why I'm so excited about [Company]'s work on..."

Don't give me a timeline. Give me a trailer for the movie of your career.

**CTA:**
How do you answer "Tell me about yourself"? Do you have a structured approach?

## Post 10: How to Talk About Failure
**Target:** All Personas (Behavioral)
**Hook:** "Tell me about a time you failed." (Hint: "I worked too hard" is not the answer.)

**Body:**
Interviewers ask about failure for one reason: They want to know if you're honest and if you grow.

If you say "I've never really failed," you're lying or lacking self-awareness.
If you give a "fake failure" (like "I care too much"), you're dodging the question.

**The Real Failure Framework:**

1. **The Mistake:** Be real. "I deployed a change that took down production for 20 minutes." "I underestimated the complexity of the migration and we missed the deadline by a week."
2. **The Ownership:** Don't blame the QA team. Don't blame the PM. "I didn't add enough integration tests." "I didn't communicate the delay early enough."
3. **The Pivot:** What did you do to fix it *in the moment*?
4. **The System Fix:** This is the most important part. "To make sure this never happened again, I implemented a pre-deploy checklist/automated the rollback process/changed our sprint planning meeting."

A failure that leads to a system improvement isn't a red flag. It's a badge of experience.

**CTA:**
What's a "failure" that actually made you a better engineer in the long run?
