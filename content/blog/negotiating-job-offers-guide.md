# Negotiating Job Offers: How to Get 20-40% More Compensation

Most engineers leave significant money on the table by not negotiating or by negotiating ineffectively. The discomfort of negotiation is real, but it's proportionally small compared to the difference between a negotiated and un-negotiated offer. A well-handled negotiation adds $10K-$50K in first-year compensation at large tech companies and creates a higher base for all future raises.

## The Fundamental Principle

Negotiation works because hiring is expensive. Replacing an offer-declined candidate costs the company weeks of recruiter time, hiring manager time, and the risk of losing the role longer. The expected cost of negotiating (offer rescinded) is essentially zero for reasonable requests from candidates who've passed the technical bar. Companies expect negotiation — an immediate acceptance signals that you underpriced yourself.

Offers are never rescinded for polite, professional counter-offers. In over a decade of observing hiring, I've never seen an offer pulled because a candidate asked for more money respectfully.

## Understanding Total Compensation

Most engineers focus on base salary but total compensation (TC) includes multiple components:

**Base salary:** Fixed cash, paid regularly. The foundation everything else builds on.

**Signing bonus:** One-time payment at joining. Good for bridging unvested equity at your current company. Doesn't repeat.

**Annual bonus:** Performance-based, typically 10-20% of base at large tech companies. Variable — don't count on target as guaranteed.

**RSUs/Stock:** Grants of company stock that vest over time (typically 4 years with 1-year cliff at tech companies). At public companies: real cash value at current stock price. At pre-IPO: illiquid, value depends on outcome.

**Benefits:** Health insurance (valued at $5K-$20K/year depending on coverage and family status), 401K match, equity refresh grants, PTO, remote work stipends.

When comparing offers, compute TC at the 4-year mark. A higher base with no equity might exceed a lower base with significant RSUs, or vice versa depending on company stage.

## The Negotiation Playbook

**Step 1: Don't reveal your target first.** When asked "What are your salary expectations?" or "What are you currently making?", deflect: "I'm open to hearing what the role pays — what's the budgeted range?" or "I'm focused on finding the right fit; I'm happy to discuss compensation once we both agree this is the right role." In many US states, employers cannot legally require your current salary.

**Step 2: Get the offer in writing.** Verbal offers are good; written offers are negotiable. Don't negotiate verbally — it's too easy to say yes in the moment.

**Step 3: Express enthusiasm before negotiating.** "I'm really excited about this opportunity and the team — thank you for the offer. I'd like to discuss the compensation to make sure we can make this work." You're not threatening to walk; you're signaling genuine interest while opening a conversation.

**Step 4: Make a specific counter.** Don't say "Is there any flexibility?" — that's too easy to say no to. Name a number: "Based on my research on levels.fyi and comparable offers, I was expecting something in the [$X-$Y] range for base. Is there flexibility to get closer to that?" Specific requests are harder to decline without explanation.

**Step 5: Counter in multiple dimensions.** If base is fixed, ask about signing bonus, RSU grant size, or an earlier performance review. "If base is the maximum, would you be able to increase the signing bonus to offset the unvested equity I'm leaving behind?"

**Step 6: Use competing offers as leverage — when you have them.** "I have another offer at $X. I'd prefer to join your company because [genuine reason], but the gap is significant. Can you help me close it?" This is the most powerful negotiation tool.

## What to Say

**Template for a counter-offer:**
> "I'm very excited about [Company] and genuinely want to make this work. Based on my research and current market rates for this level and location, I was hoping to land closer to $[X] in base salary. Is there flexibility to get there, or to offset the difference through a larger signing bonus or equity grant?"

**Handling "this is our best offer":** They're often not done. "I understand budget constraints are real — I appreciate that. Could you check if there's any flexibility on the signing bonus or the equity refresh policy?" Different levers.

**Handling competing offers:** Be honest about the existence of competing offers. Don't reveal the competing company unless you're comfortable with them potentially contacting the other company (rare, but possible at executive levels).

## Common Mistakes

**Accepting verbally before negotiating:** Once you say "I accept," the negotiation is over. Never accept on the first call.

**Revealing your current salary:** Not legally required in most US states. Your current salary is a ceiling on your first offer.

**Negotiating only on base:** Total comp is what matters. A $10K base increase at 22% tax is ~$7,800/year. A $30K signing bonus is $25,200 after tax in year one.

**Underestimating your leverage:** If you've received an offer, the company has already invested significantly and wants you to join. Your negotiation leverage is at its peak between offer and acceptance.

**Not having a number in mind:** Know your target TC, your walk-away number, and the breakpoints between them. Negotiating without a target produces random outcomes.

The engineers who negotiate confidently are not necessarily more technical or more impressive — they simply understand that negotiation is a normal, expected part of accepting an offer, and they've prepared for the conversation.
