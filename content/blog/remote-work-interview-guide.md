# How to Interview for Remote-First Engineering Roles: What Distributed Companies Test Differently

Getting hired at a remote-first company isn't just about passing the same technical interview with a video call instead of a whiteboard. Companies built around distributed teams — GitLab, Automattic, Basecamp, Stripe, Shopify, and many others — have genuinely different engineering cultures, and their interviews probe for attributes that in-person companies often don't prioritize.

If you're targeting remote-first roles, you need to understand what these companies are actually evaluating, and prepare accordingly.

## Why Remote-First Interviews Are Different

Co-located teams can compensate for gaps in written communication, proactive transparency, and self-direction with proximity. You can tap someone on the shoulder, read body language in a meeting, or overhear context. Remote teams have none of that. Every gap in async communication is a real problem.

This means remote-first companies screen for different things: written clarity, self-management, asynchronous decision-making, and a demonstrated ability to produce output without real-time oversight. Technical skills matter, but so does your ability to operate effectively without being in the same room as your team.

## What Remote-First Companies Test

### 1. Written Communication Quality

At GitLab (entirely remote, 2,000+ employees), the engineering interview process explicitly assesses written communication. Candidates often complete take-home exercises, write technical proposals, or are evaluated on the quality of their written follow-ups to interviews.

**What they're looking for:** Can you explain a technical decision clearly and concisely in writing? Can you write a proposal that a colleague can review asynchronously and understand without a verbal explanation?

**How to prepare:** Review your past written communication. Practice writing technical explanations that don't rely on verbal caveats ("as I mentioned earlier," "you know what I mean"). Read GitLab's public handbook — their engineering section shows exactly what "good" written communication looks like in a distributed company.

### 2. Asynchronous Collaboration Experience

Remote-first companies move through decisions in writing — GitHub comments, Notion documents, Slack threads that don't require immediate responses. Candidates who've only worked synchronously often struggle with this.

**What they ask:** "Describe how you've worked with teammates in different time zones." "How do you make decisions when you can't get real-time input?" "Tell me about a technical debate you had asynchronously — how was it resolved?"

**What strong answers look like:** "I documented the trade-offs, shared the write-up in our engineering Slack channel, gave the team 48 hours to comment, synthesized the feedback, and made a call." Not: "I scheduled a meeting to discuss it."

### 3. Self-Direction and Proactive Communication

At a co-located company, managers can see when you're stuck. At a remote company, you're invisible until you communicate. Remote-first companies are paranoid about engineers who go quiet.

**What they look for:** Do you proactively share blockers? Do you document your decisions? Do you update stakeholders without being asked?

**Behavioral questions to expect:** "Tell me about a time you were blocked on a task. How did you handle it?" "How do you keep your team informed about your progress?" Strong answers demonstrate that you default to over-communication, not under-communication.

### 4. Output Orientation Over Process Orientation

Remote teams can't manage by presence. They manage by output. Remote-first companies hire engineers who are comfortable being evaluated on what they ship, not how many hours they were visible in a Slack channel.

**Interview signal:** They'll ask about specific projects you completed, the impact of your work, and how you measured success. They're less interested in "I was part of the team that built X" and more interested in "I owned X, shipped it in 6 weeks, and it reduced latency by 30%."

**How to prepare:** Reframe your STAR stories around concrete outcomes. Instead of describing what you did, describe what changed as a result. Numbers help: lines of code is less interesting than percentage improvement in system performance.

### 5. Timezone and Schedule Transparency

Remote companies often have teams spanning many time zones. Interviewers may probe how you handle overlap challenges — not to disqualify you if you're in a difficult timezone, but to understand how you'd adapt.

**Be prepared to discuss:** How you manage your working hours when your team is distributed across timezones. Whether you're comfortable with async reviews and delayed feedback. How you've made decisions when synchronous input wasn't immediately available.

## The Take-Home Exercise: A Remote-Specific Assessment

Many remote-first companies use take-home exercises as a primary technical assessment. This is itself a signal: remote work is built on asynchronous contribution, and a take-home exercise simulates that environment.

**Common formats:**
- A coding exercise to be completed over 3–7 days
- A technical design document to be written and submitted for review
- A code review exercise where you provide written feedback on provided code

**How to approach take-homes:** Treat them like real work. Write clean, documented code. Add comments explaining non-obvious decisions. If you make trade-offs, note them explicitly. Structure any written component as you would a real technical document — with a summary, context, proposal, and considered alternatives.

This is where many candidates lose remote-first roles. They treat take-homes like LeetCode exercises and submit minimal code without context. Remote-first companies want to see how you'd actually work with them.

## Logistics and Professionalism for Remote Interviews

Your remote interview environment signals how you'd work day-to-day.

**Before the interview:** Test your audio and video. A lagging video call is forgiven; audio that cuts out is a red flag about your home setup. Use a wired connection if possible. Have your IDE or coding environment ready.

**During the interview:** More so than in-person, you must compensate for the absence of physical presence. Be explicit about your thinking — narrate your approach before diving in. Check in frequently: "Does that answer your question, or would you like me to go deeper?"

**After the interview:** Send a follow-up email. This is standard advice for all interviews, but at remote companies it carries more weight — it's another data point on your written communication.

## Companies Known for Remote-First Hiring

Each has distinct interview characteristics worth researching:

- **GitLab:** Strongly values handbook familiarity; explicitly evaluates async communication
- **Automattic:** Writing exercises are central; values individual contributor autonomy
- **Stripe:** Remote-friendly (not pure remote-first); strong emphasis on written design docs internally
- **Shopify:** Distributed teams; values shipping speed and clear decision-making
- **Buffer, Doist, Remote.com:** Smaller teams; cultural fit for async-first communication is weighted heavily

## Summary

Remote-first interview preparation isn't separate from technical preparation — it's additive. You still need strong algorithms, system design, and behavioral stories. But you also need to demonstrate:

- Written communication clarity
- Comfort with async collaboration
- Proactive transparency about blockers and decisions
- Strong output orientation

The engineers who thrive in remote-first companies — and in their interviews — are those who can operate independently, communicate deliberately, and measure themselves by what they ship rather than where they sit.
