# Advanced Salary Negotiation: Competing Offers, Equity, and Compensation Strategy

Most salary negotiation guides cover the basics: always negotiate, don't name a number first, express enthusiasm. This guide assumes you know that. It covers the tactics that matter most for senior engineers and people evaluating complex compensation packages — competing offers, equity structures, and the math behind total compensation.

## Using Competing Offers Strategically

A competing offer is the most powerful tool in a negotiation. It converts a subjective conversation ("I think I'm worth more") into an objective market data point. Companies know this, which is why the question "do you have other offers?" comes up in almost every negotiation.

Timing matters. Reveal a competing offer after you have a written offer from the company you prefer, not before. This avoids being screened out early for being "too expensive" and lets you negotiate from a position of genuine choice rather than theoretical demand.

Be truthful. Fabricating or significantly inflating a competing offer is a small professional world risk — recruiters talk to each other, and some companies do call to verify. You do not have to share every detail of the competing offer, but the numbers you state should be real.

The script: "I've received another offer from [company] at [total comp or base]. I'm more excited about this role, but I need to be able to make the numbers work. Is there flexibility to bring the offer closer to that range?" This is non-adversarial and gives the recruiter something concrete to take to their compensation team.

If the company you prefer cannot match on base salary, ask what they can move on: signing bonus, equity grant, accelerated vesting, title, or remote flexibility. Expanding the negotiation surface often unlocks movement where a direct base salary ask would be refused.

## Forcing a Timeline

Companies use urgency against candidates ("we need a decision by Friday"). You can use timeline symmetry in the other direction. If you have an offer deadline from one company and are waiting on another, email the preferred company: "I have an offer with an expiration date of [date] and want to give you the opportunity to complete your process before I need to decide." This is professional and often accelerates their timeline or prompts a faster-than-expected offer.

Do not accept an offer and then continue interviewing. Do not accept and immediately renege — this damages your reputation in ways that can follow you. But asking for a reasonable extension (5–10 business days) is standard and almost always granted.

## Equity Deep Dive: RSUs vs. Options

Equity is often the largest component of senior compensation at growth companies, yet it is the least understood. The two primary forms are RSUs and stock options, and they have fundamentally different risk and reward profiles.

**Restricted Stock Units (RSUs)** are a promise to deliver shares on a vesting schedule. They have value as long as the company has any positive stock price. At public companies, RSUs vest and become immediately sellable (minus tax). At private companies, RSUs vest but you cannot sell until a liquidity event — meaning years of compensation you cannot access.

**Stock options** give you the right to buy shares at a fixed price (the strike price, set at fair market value when granted). They only have value if the company's stock price rises above the strike price. For options, you need to understand: the strike price (lower is better), the current 409A valuation (for private companies), and the spread (how much above strike the current value is).

**Cliffs and vesting schedules**: The standard schedule is a one-year cliff followed by monthly or quarterly vesting over three more years (1/4 vests at the cliff, then 1/48 per month for 36 more months). The cliff means leaving before 12 months nets you zero equity. Negotiate to waive the cliff if you are joining from a strong position, or ask for accelerated vesting upon acquisition (double-trigger acceleration: change of control + involuntary termination).

**Refresh grants**: At high-performing companies, refresh grants are issued annually or biannually to retain employees as the initial grant vests. Ask about the refresh cadence and typical amounts during negotiation — a stingy refresh policy means your total equity declines toward zero after year four.

## Reading Total Compensation

Total comp = base salary + cash bonus (target % of base) + equity (annualized grant value) + benefits (health, 401k match, etc.) + one-time components (signing bonus).

Signing bonuses are often used to make up for unvested equity you forfeit by leaving your current employer (a "golden handcuff buyout"). They are typically clawed back if you leave within one to two years — read the terms.

When comparing offers, annualize the equity grant over the vesting period. A $400,000 equity grant vesting over four years is $100,000 per year, but that assumes the stock price stays constant. For public companies with liquid stock, apply a discount for price uncertainty. For private companies, equity value is speculative — weight it at 20–30% of face value unless you have strong conviction about the company's trajectory.

## BATNA and the Walk-Away Point

BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) is the outcome you fall back on if the negotiation fails. For a job negotiation, your BATNA is usually your current job or another offer. A strong BATNA gives you credibility and calm — it is genuinely easier to negotiate when you are not desperate.

Set your walk-away point before you enter any negotiation conversation. What is the minimum total comp at which you would accept this role, accounting for all components? This prevents in-the-moment pressure from causing you to accept something you will regret. If the company's final offer is below that number, decline professionally and move on.

Negotiating remotely (email vs. phone) gives you more time to think but less ability to read the other party. Use email for initial anchoring and requests; use calls for resolving ambiguity and closing. Always confirm verbal agreements in writing by summarizing the call in an email.

## Counter-Offer from Your Current Employer

If your employer makes a counter-offer when you resign, think carefully before accepting. Statistically, most employees who accept counter-offers leave within 12–18 months anyway — the underlying reasons for looking (compensation, growth, culture) have not changed, and your loyalty is now in question. The counter-offer is often a retention tactic that buys time to find your replacement.

The exception: if your primary motivation for looking was compensation alone and the counter-offer fully addresses that, staying is a reasonable choice. But if the reasons ran deeper, the counter-offer does not solve the real problem.
