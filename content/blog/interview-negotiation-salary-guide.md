# Salary Negotiation for Engineers: A Tactical Guide

Salary negotiation is the highest-leverage activity in your career, and most engineers are terrible at it. A single negotiation can be worth $20,000–$100,000 in additional first-year compensation — and far more over a career, since future raises are anchored to your current base. This guide gives you a practical framework for evaluating offers, using competing offers as leverage, and the specific language to use in negotiation conversations.

## Understanding Total Compensation

Never evaluate an offer by base salary alone. Total compensation (TC) for a software engineer typically has three components:

**Base salary** is the guaranteed cash component. It's the most liquid and least risky part of your offer. It's also what future raises, 401k matches, and severance calculations are anchored to.

**Equity** is the most variable and most misunderstood component. At public companies, equity is typically granted as Restricted Stock Units (RSUs). At private companies, it's usually stock options (ISOs or NSOs). To evaluate equity in an offer, you need: the number of shares or units, the vesting schedule (see below), the current fair market value, and — for private companies — the fully diluted share count (to compute your ownership percentage). Never accept a private company equity grant without understanding the strike price, the most recent 409A valuation, and the preferred vs common share stack.

**Bonus** comes in two forms: signing bonuses (one-time, often with a clawback clause requiring repayment if you leave within 1–2 years) and annual performance bonuses (variable, based on company and individual performance). A "target bonus of 15%" is not the same as "you will receive 15% of your base every year" — in a bad year, that target might pay out at 0–50%.

When comparing two offers, convert everything to an annualized TC figure. For a 4-year RSU grant, divide the total grant value by 4. Add that to base plus expected bonus. Use today's stock price for public company RSUs; use the 409A for private company options (with heavy skepticism — most private equity is worth nothing at exit).

## The Cliff, Vesting Schedule, and Why They Matter

Standard vesting for both RSUs and options is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. This means: you receive 0 equity if you leave before 12 months of employment. At the 12-month mark, 25% vests at once (the "cliff"). The remaining 75% vests monthly or quarterly over the next 3 years.

The cliff creates a significant asymmetry: the company bears almost no risk if you leave in month 11 — you've done nearly a year of work and received no equity. This is intentional. As a negotiator, you should account for this by:

1. Treating year-1 TC as lower than advertised (your RSUs aren't truly yours until month 12)
2. Asking for a shorter cliff (6 months is sometimes negotiable at smaller companies)
3. Negotiating a higher signing bonus to compensate for unvested equity you're leaving at your current employer

If you're leaving unvested equity at your current company, quantify it precisely and use it as a direct negotiation lever: "I have $45K in unvested RSUs that vest in February. To make this transition financially neutral for me, I need a signing bonus that covers that amount."

## Using Competing Offers as Leverage

A competing offer is the single most powerful negotiating tool you have. It transforms the conversation from "what are you worth?" to "what will it take to win?" Companies respond to competitive pressure in ways they simply don't respond to abstract requests for more.

To use competing offers effectively:

**Be truthful.** Lying about a competing offer — or inventing one — is a form of fraud that can result in offer rescission if discovered. Only cite real offers with real numbers.

**Be specific but not fully transparent.** You don't have to reveal the company name. "I have a competing offer from a well-known fintech company for $195K base with a $300K RSU grant" is more credible than vague claims, but doesn't require you to name the competitor.

**Time it right.** Share the competing offer after you've received an offer but before you've expressed strong excitement. The sequence: receive offer → express genuine interest → mention the competing offer → ask what they can do.

**Give them a chance to respond.** "I'm really excited about this role and I'd prefer to join your team. But I want to be honest that I have a competing offer at $X that I need to seriously consider. Is there any flexibility on the equity or base?" This phrasing communicates preference for them while creating urgency.

## Negotiation Scripts and When to Push Back

The most important principle in negotiation: **never say yes to the first offer.** Even if the offer exceeds your expectations, ask for something. Companies expect negotiation and almost always have headroom.

Phrases that work:

- "Thank you for the offer — I'm excited about the opportunity. Before I decide, I want to make sure we're aligned on compensation. Is there flexibility on the base?" (Opener — no specifics yet, tests for wiggle room.)
- "I was hoping to be at $[X]. Is that something we can work toward?" (Anchor to your target number, not their offer.)
- "The base is a bit below what I was expecting based on market data for this role and level. Can you get to $[X]?" (Cites external data rather than personal desire — less adversarial.)
- "If the base is firm, would you be able to increase the RSU grant?" (Pivots negotiation to a different lever when one is exhausted.)
- "I need a few days to review the full package with my family before responding." (Buys time without closing the negotiation.)

When to push back hard: if the offer is below the 50th percentile for the role in your market (use levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and Blind for public data), or if you have a competing offer significantly higher. When to accept gracefully: when you've exhausted meaningful levers and the offer is genuinely competitive, or when non-compensation factors (mission, team, growth trajectory) meaningfully favor the role.

## Evaluating RSUs vs Options

RSUs and options are very different instruments. RSUs are straightforward — at vesting, you receive shares worth their current market price, and you owe income tax on that value. Options require you to pay the strike price to acquire shares, and you only profit if the stock price exceeds the strike price. Options have more upside (you're essentially holding a call option) but more risk (they can expire worthless).

For public company options: calculate the spread (current price minus strike price) and compare to the RSU value for the same number of shares. Options at a company trading near their strike price are nearly worthless relative to RSUs.

For private company options: the key question is whether the exit valuation will be high enough, given the liquidation preferences of preferred shareholders, for common stock (which options convert to) to receive meaningful proceeds. In a 1x liquidation preference stack with a $500M exit on a $400M preferred investment, common shareholders may receive nothing. Ask for the cap table, the preference stack, and the break-even exit valuation before accepting any private company equity offer.
