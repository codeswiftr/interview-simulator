# How to Handle a Bad Interviewer

Most interview advice assumes a competent, engaged interviewer who wants to understand your thinking. Many interviews do not work that way. Interviewers cancel without notice, ask ambiguous questions, interrupt constantly, check their phone, or ask questions that have no good answer. This guide is about what to do when the interviewer is the problem.

## The Disengaged Interviewer

The most common bad interviewer is simply not present. They are distracted, give minimal feedback, respond in monosyllables, and seem to be waiting for the interview to end.

**What is happening**: They may be having a bad day, they may not want to be doing interviews, or the company's interview culture may be poor and they were volunteered. It is rarely about you.

**What to do**: Keep your energy up regardless of their engagement level. The interview is still happening. Ask direct questions to draw them in: "Does that approach make sense to you?" or "Is there a specific constraint I should focus on first?" Engaged questions create engagement.

Do not mirror their energy downward. A candidate who goes flat when the interviewer is flat is easy to miss. A candidate who stays curious and energetic despite a low-energy interviewer is noticed — sometimes favorably by the interviewer themselves when they debrief later.

## The Hostile Interviewer

Some interviewers push back on correct answers, dismiss your reasoning, or seem determined to make you wrong. This is usually a stress test, sometimes bad interviewing, occasionally both.

**What is happening**: Stress tests (common at Goldman Sachs, Jane Street, some trading firms) deliberately apply pressure to see how candidates respond. The interviewer may also genuinely disagree with your answer — which is worth engaging with.

**What to do**: Stay calm and stand your ground when you are correct. "I understand your concern — let me think through whether my assumption holds." If you think you are right, defend it: "I may be missing something, but here is why I believe this approach works: [explanation]. What specifically seems wrong to you?"

The worst response to a stress test is to capitulate immediately. Interviewers who push back want to see whether you can hold your position under pressure. Folding at the first challenge signals low conviction, which is a worse signal than getting a problem slightly wrong.

If after their clarification you realize you were wrong, update your position clearly: "That's a good point — you're right that [X] changes the analysis. Let me revise."

## The Unclear or Disorganized Interviewer

Some interviewers ask questions that are ambiguous, internally inconsistent, or missing critical information. Sometimes the question itself is bad.

**What to do**: Ask clarifying questions, then restate your understanding before proceeding. "Before I start, let me make sure I understand the problem: [restatement]. Is that correct?"

If you are halfway through a solution and realize the problem as stated has no good answer, say so: "I'm finding that this approach leads to [contradiction]. Can you help me understand whether that is a constraint I should work around or whether I've misunderstood something?"

Most disorganized interviewers respect candidates who can impose structure on an ambiguous problem. That is a real engineering skill. Asking good clarifying questions in an unclear interview is not a sign of weakness — it is a demonstration of how you handle ambiguous requirements.

## The Interviewer Who Talks Too Much

Some interviewers spend 15 minutes explaining context before asking the question, interrupt your answers to riff on related points, or turn every answer into a lecture about their experience.

**What to do**: Use active listening signals to encourage them to reach the point ("yes," "right," nodding) without encouraging them to continue indefinitely. When you have a chance, redirect toward the question: "That's interesting context. Given that, what specifically would you like me to focus on?"

Keep your own answers focused and direct. Long-winded interviewers often respond well to concise answers because it creates space for them to talk.

At the end of the interview, make sure to ask your questions clearly — this signals that you have substance beyond waiting for them to finish.

## The Interviewer Who Expects a Specific Answer

Some interviewers have a particular solution in mind and are not receptive to different correct approaches. They may hint that you are wrong even when you are not, or redirect you repeatedly toward their preferred solution.

**What is happening**: The interview may have been designed around one solution. The interviewer may lack the expertise to recognize an alternative correct approach.

**What to do**: When you feel hints pushing you toward a specific direction, engage with it: "Are you suggesting I should approach this with [specific technique]?" If their preferred approach is also valid, consider switching — not because yours is wrong, but because demonstrating you can follow their reasoning also has value.

If their preferred approach is significantly worse than yours, you can make the case diplomatically: "I can walk through [their approach] — I'd be curious what you see as the advantage over [your approach], since I was concerned about [trade-off]."

## After a Bad Interview

Bad interviews are information. An interviewer who was hostile or disorganized reflects something about the company's culture. An interviewer who clearly was not prepared suggests something about how the company runs processes.

If you still want the role after a bad interview, you can address it directly with the recruiter: "I wanted to flag that the interview with [name] felt different from the others — I'm not sure whether I communicated my thinking well in that format, and I wanted to make sure the panel has a complete picture."

This is not complaining. It is providing information. Recruiters often know which interviewers are inconsistent and will take your note seriously.

If the culture the bad interview revealed is not one you want to work in, the interview saved you from accepting the wrong job. That is the most useful outcome a bad interview can produce.
