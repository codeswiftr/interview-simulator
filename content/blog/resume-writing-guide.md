# Software Engineer Resumé Writing Guide

A software engineering resume has one job: get you a phone screen. It's not a biography, a skills inventory, or a creative expression — it's a signal-to-noise optimization problem where the signal is "this person is worth talking to" and the noise is everything else. Most resumes fail because they're written from the perspective of describing work done rather than demonstrating impact delivered. Understanding what engineering hiring managers and recruiters actually look at, in what order, and for how long shapes every element of an effective resume.

## The 6-Second Reality

Recruiters and hiring managers typically spend 6-10 seconds on a first pass of a resume. In that time, they're scanning for: company names, title/level, graduation year, and 1-2 highlighted accomplishments. Your entire resume must be optimized for this first pass — the structure must make the key signals immediately visible, or the resume doesn't get a second look.

**Single page up to 7-8 years of experience**: After 8-10 years, two pages are acceptable. More than two pages is almost always wrong for software engineers. If you're struggling to fit experience on one page, the problem is not lack of space — it's including too many low-signal entries.

**Clean formatting, no columns for experience**: Multi-column layouts disrupt ATS parsing and make scanning harder. Experience entries should be in a single-column list. Columns are acceptable for a skills section or brief summary.

## Experience Entries: Impact, Not Responsibility

The most important writing improvement for most resumes:

**Wrong** (responsibility): *"Responsible for maintaining the user authentication service and working with the team to add new features."*

**Right** (impact): *"Reduced authentication service P99 latency by 40% (280ms → 170ms) by introducing connection pooling and query optimization; supports 200K DAU."*

Every experience bullet should follow the pattern: **action verb + what you did + quantified result**. Numbers are compelling: percentage improvements, absolute scale, time saved, users impacted, dollars enabled. If you don't have exact numbers, reasonable estimates are better than vague descriptions.

**Strong action verbs**: Led, Architected, Reduced, Improved, Built, Designed, Optimized, Shipped, Scaled, Automated. Weak verbs: Helped, Assisted, Worked on, Participated in.

**Scale context**: "Reduced database query time by 50%" means more with context: "Reduced database query time by 50% for a service handling 50M requests/day." Engineers who understand scale know the difference between optimizing a hobby project and a production system.

## Structure

**Contact and header**: Name, email, LinkedIn URL, GitHub URL (if active), location (city/state or "Remote"). Phone number optional. Do not include: photo, date of birth, full address.

**Summary (optional, high-impact when done well)**: 2-3 sentences for senior engineers. "Senior software engineer with 8 years of experience in distributed systems and database engineering. Built and scaled infrastructure at [Company A] from 1M to 50M users. Strong in Go, PostgreSQL, and Kubernetes." This helps recruiters quickly classify you. Skip the summary if it would be generic.

**Experience** (reverse chronological): Company, title, dates, location. 3-5 bullets per role. Focus on the highest-impact work — not everything you did, but the things that most demonstrate your ability to do the target role.

**Technical skills**: Not a comprehensive list of every technology you've touched — a focused list of what you're strongest in and what you want to use. Organize by category: Languages, Frameworks, Infrastructure, Databases. Listing 40 technologies reads as padding.

**Education**: Degree, institution, graduation year. For engineers 5+ years out of school, education moves to the bottom. For bootcamp graduates, mention the bootcamp explicitly (don't hide it — it's neutral to positive at most companies).

**Projects (for junior/bootcamp candidates)**: 2-3 highlighted projects with links. Name, one-sentence description, tech stack, notable aspect. Skip this section once you have 3+ years of professional experience.

## Handling Specific Situations

**Employment gaps**: A gap of 1-2 years can be explained with a brief note ("Career break: parenting, personal health, travel") or left to the cover letter/phone screen. Trying to hide gaps (using "years only" for dates, fabricating roles) is worse than explaining them honestly.

**Non-traditional background**: List bootcamp as education. Frame career change explicitly and briefly: "Former UX designer; transitioned to software engineering via [Bootcamp] in 2023." Trying to hide the transition often results in inconsistent resume narrative that raises more questions.

**Multiple short tenures**: If you've had several 1-year roles, grouping them or providing brief context for each is better than hoping the interviewer doesn't notice. "Contract role at X," "startup was acquired," or "team was eliminated" are all legitimate context.

**International experience**: International companies and experience are generally positive signals. No need to explain or apologize for international credentials.

## ATS Optimization

Most large companies use Applicant Tracking Systems to screen resumes before human review:

**Use the job description's language**: If the job description mentions "distributed systems," use "distributed systems" not "large-scale systems." ATS matching is often keyword-based.

**Avoid tables, text boxes, and headers/footers**: ATS parsers often can't read these. Stick to standard document structure.

**Submit as PDF**: Preserves formatting across systems. Some older ATS systems parse .docx better; if a job application specifically requests .docx, provide it.

**Don't optimize obsessively**: ATS systems are getting better and human review follows. An excellent resume that a slightly suboptimal ATS score is better than a resume stuffed with keywords but less compelling to humans.

## What Not to Include

**Objective statement**: Never. Every engineer's objective is "get this job."
**References available upon request**: Assumed; wastes space.
**"Proficient in Microsoft Office"**: Not a differentiating skill for engineers.
**High school education**: Remove once you have any college/bootcamp credential.
**Photographs**: Standard in some countries but not US/Canada/UK tech hiring; can introduce bias.

A well-written resume is 2-4 hours of work to get right. Given that it determines whether you get screened in or out for every job you apply to, it's among the highest-ROI time investments in a job search.
