# Bootcamp to Software Engineer Interview Guide

Coding bootcamps have placed thousands of engineers at real companies. The path from bootcamp to employed engineer is well-established, but it requires a different strategy than the traditional CS degree path. Employers have calibrated to bootcamp graduates — some actively recruit from them, others screen skeptically. Understanding how hiring works for bootcamp candidates, and positioning your experience correctly, makes the difference between a 3-month job search and a 12-month one.

## The Honest Landscape

**Who hires bootcamp graduates**: Primarily startups, mid-size growth companies, and companies with explicit junior engineer programs. Large tech companies (FAANG) rarely hire bootcamp graduates directly into engineering roles — their bars are calibrated to CS graduates with competitive programming preparation. This is not a permanent ceiling; many engineers move into large tech after 2-3 years of industry experience.

**What interviewers assess**: At the junior level, technical aptitude (can you learn quickly), execution (can you ship working code), and culture fit matter more than depth of knowledge. Interviewers for junior roles know you don't know everything — they're assessing your trajectory, not your current state.

**The bias exists and can be addressed**: Some interviewers have negative priors about bootcamp graduates. The most effective counter-evidence is a strong portfolio with deployed, working projects and clear technical communication.

## Building a Portfolio That Works

**3-4 deployed projects, not 10 unfinished ones**: Quality over quantity. Each project should be live (accessible URL), functional (all the obvious paths work), and interesting enough to discuss for 10 minutes. Employers can't evaluate a project they can't run.

**Show technical depth in at least one project**: One project should demonstrate something beyond the bootcamp curriculum: integration with an external API, a non-trivial backend feature, some algorithmic work, or a performance optimization you can explain. This "depth project" signals that you pushed beyond the program.

**Clean repositories**: GitHub presence with clean repositories signals professionalism. README files that explain what the project does, how to run it locally, and what technical decisions were made. Commit history that shows iterative development (not one giant commit). Avoid committing credentials or `.env` files.

**Document your process**: Blog posts about technical decisions you made, problems you solved, or things you learned are uncommon among junior candidates and strongly differentiate. "I built X and here's what I learned about Y" posts signal a learning mindset that employers want to see in junior hires.

## Passing Technical Screens

**Algorithms and data structures gaps**: Bootcamps typically cover web development, not algorithms. Companies above early startup stage often include LeetCode-style questions. You need to cover: arrays/strings, hash maps, basic sorting, linked lists, stacks/queues, and recursion. LeetCode easy questions should be solvable; LeetCode medium is the target for more competitive roles.

**Study timeline**: 6-8 weeks of consistent algorithm practice (1 hour/day) is enough to be competitive for junior roles. Neetcode 150 is the most efficient structured curriculum. Don't try to memorize solutions — practice until you can recognize patterns and explain approaches.

**Be explicit about your learning**: Junior interviewers expect knowledge gaps. "I haven't implemented a trie before, but let me think through how it would work based on what I know about trees" is a better response than silence or guessing. Demonstrating how you learn in real-time is a signal employers value.

**Web-specific technical depth**: Your strongest technical ground is the stack you built in. If you built in React + Node, know React hooks, async/await, REST API design, basic database queries, and the browser's event loop. Going deep on what you built beats shallow coverage of what you didn't.

## The Interview Strategy

**Target your applications**: Apply to companies that hire bootcamp graduates and have junior engineering programs. Built In, AngelList, and job boards at coding bootcamp career centers (Lambda School/Bloomtech, Fullstack Academy, etc.) surface bootcamp-friendly employers. Ask your bootcamp's career team which employers have hired from your cohort.

**Referrals matter more for bootcamp grads**: Without institutional credential signals, a referral from someone inside the company carries disproportionate weight. Network actively — reach out to bootcamp alumni who are now working engineers, attend local tech meetups, connect on LinkedIn and ask for informational calls. People hired from bootcamps often pay it forward.

**Prepare your narrative**: The story "I left X to do a bootcamp because I wanted to transition into engineering, and here's what I've built since" needs to be crisp, confident, and forward-looking. You chose this path intentionally. The pivot is a feature of your story, not a weakness to apologize for.

**Frame your prior experience**: Non-engineering experience is often an asset that bootcamp candidates undervalue. A former teacher brings clear communication and patience with ambiguity. A former operations manager brings process thinking. A former healthcare worker brings domain expertise if applying to health tech. Connect your prior experience to how you think about engineering.

## After Your First Role

The single biggest career move for bootcamp graduates: after 18-24 months of industry experience, your bootcamp history becomes irrelevant. You have shipped code in production. You have worked in a real engineering team. You have on-the-job experience that CS graduates in their first role also don't have. Two years of experience opens doors that were closed to bootcamp candidates with no experience.

Invest in the fundamentals during your first job: CS concepts (operating systems, networking, data structures) fill in the gaps over time through targeted reading (Designing Data-Intensive Applications, Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces). These aren't urgent on day one, but they compound.

The bootcamp-to-engineer path is real, well-traveled, and accelerating. The engineers who navigate it best combine portfolio depth, targeted job search strategy, and a learning mindset that makes interviewers want to invest in their development.
