# The Best Questions to Ask Your Interviewers: What to Ask at Each Stage

Every interview ends with some version of the same invitation: "Do you have any questions for us?" This is not a formality. It is one of the highest-leverage moments in the entire process, and most candidates waste it.

Asking thoughtful questions does three things simultaneously: it signals genuine engagement, it helps you evaluate whether the role is actually right for you, and it surfaces information that no job description will ever contain. The right questions at the right stage can shift the interviewer's impression of you from "competent candidate" to "the person who clearly thought this through."

Here is what to ask — and when.

## Phone Screen: Clarify Scope and Signal Early

The phone screen is typically short and informational. Your interviewer (often a recruiter or hiring manager) is assessing whether you clear a basic bar before investing more time. Your questions here should be efficient and focused on understanding the role accurately.

**Questions that work well:**

- "Can you walk me through what a typical sprint or week looks like for this role?"
- "How is this team structured — what's the ratio of engineers to product managers and designers?"
- "Is this a backfill or a new headcount, and what does that tell us about team growth direction?"
- "What does success look like at the six-month mark for someone in this role?"

The last question is particularly useful early. The answer reveals whether the company has thought concretely about the role or is still figuring it out. Vague answers ("just getting ramped up") at a growth-stage company may be fine; vague answers at a late-stage company with mature processes are a yellow flag.

## Technical Rounds: Probe Engineering Culture

When you are talking to engineers on the team, shift to questions about how the team actually works. Engineers are often more candid than managers, especially about technical debt, deployment friction, and on-call reality.

**Questions that land well:**

- "What does your deployment process look like — how often does the team ship to production?"
- "What's the hardest technical problem the team has faced in the last six months?"
- "How do you handle on-call? Is there a rotation, and what's the typical alert volume?"
- "If you could change one thing about the codebase or the tech stack today, what would it be?"
- "How does code review work here — what does a PR lifecycle look like?"

The question about what they would change is especially valuable. Engineers who have thought carefully about the codebase will give you a specific, honest answer. Engineers who deflect or say "nothing, it's great" either haven't thought about it or are uncomfortable being candid — both are useful data points.

## Hiring Manager Round: Understand Growth and Team Dynamics

The hiring manager conversation is where you evaluate leadership, team health, and your own growth trajectory. Managers expect more strategic questions here.

**Questions to ask:**

- "How do you think about career growth for engineers on this team — what does a path to staff or principal look like?"
- "What are the biggest challenges facing the team right now, and how is leadership prioritizing them?"
- "How do you handle disagreements between engineering and product on priority?"
- "Can you tell me about someone on the team who has grown significantly since joining — what made that happen?"
- "What's your own approach to giving feedback, and how often do you hold 1:1s?"

The story about team member growth is often the most revealing. Strong managers tend to have specific examples ready. If the answer is generic or the manager struggles to recall specific people, that tells you something about how much they invest in their team's development.

## Final-Round and Panel Interviews: Evaluate Culture and Fit

By this stage, you have a reasonable picture of the role. Use final-round questions to pressure-test what you've learned and close remaining gaps.

- "I've talked to several people on the team now — are there meaningful differences in how they'd describe the engineering culture, or does it feel consistent to you?"
- "What's the last significant process or tooling change the team made, and how was that decision made?"
- "How does the engineering org relate to the business — do engineers have input on roadmap direction?"
- "What's the attrition pattern been like — are people leaving, and if so, why?"

The attrition question is one that candidates often avoid asking because it feels awkward. Ask it anyway. Interviewers who have been through a rough patch will usually be honest, and the answer calibrates everything else you've heard.

## Offer Stage: Cover Equity, Expectations, and Start Logistics

Once you have an offer, you shift from evaluation to negotiation and logistics. Questions here are practical.

- "Can you walk me through the equity component — number of options, strike price, current 409A valuation, and vesting schedule?"
- "What is the standard expectation for in-office days, and how flexible is that?"
- "Is there a performance review cycle, and when would the first one apply to me?"
- "Is there anything about the offer that has flexibility?"

On equity specifically: always ask for the number of fully diluted shares outstanding alongside your grant size. Percentage ownership matters more than raw option count, and companies vary enormously in how proactively they share this.

## Questions Never to Ask

A few categories reliably backfire:

**Salary before the offer stage** — once you are at offer stage, compensation questions are expected. Earlier, they can signal you are focused on the wrong thing.

**Questions you could have answered with basic research** — asking what the company does, or who the CEO is, signals that you did not prepare.

**Questions that are really objections** — "I heard the on-call load is intense, is that true?" framed this way puts the interviewer on the defensive. Instead: "Can you tell me more about how on-call is structured?"

**Multi-part questions** — "Can you tell me about the tech stack, the team structure, the roadmap, and the review process?" Pick one.

## The Underlying Principle

Every question you ask is a signal. The best interviewers notice what you care about based on what you choose to explore. Candidates who ask about growth, team dynamics, and technical quality tend to be taken more seriously as future colleagues than candidates who ask only about salary and vacation policy.

Prepare four to six questions before each round, prioritized by what you most need to know. You probably will not get to all of them — and that is fine. The goal is genuine curiosity, not checklist completion.
