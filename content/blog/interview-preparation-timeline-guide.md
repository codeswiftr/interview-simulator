# 90-Day Interview Preparation Timeline: A Structured Plan for Landing Your Next Role

Interview preparation without a plan often stalls in month one. You open LeetCode, solve a few Easy problems, get discouraged by Mediums, and lose momentum before you ever touch system design or behavioral prep. A structured 90-day timeline prevents that drift by giving each phase a clear purpose, concrete milestones, and a natural progression that mirrors how real interview loops are structured.

This guide assumes you are preparing for mid-to-senior software engineering roles at product companies. Adjust the intensity and depth to match your target companies and the time you can realistically invest each week.

## Month 1: Foundations — Algorithms, Data Structures, and Core CS

The first month belongs to fundamentals. Even experienced engineers frequently have gaps in their algorithmic knowledge or have forgotten the nuances of data structure trade-offs. The goal is not to memorize solutions but to build pattern recognition that transfers to novel problems.

**Weeks 1–2: Core data structures.** Cover arrays, hash maps, linked lists, stacks, queues, and trees thoroughly. For each structure, be able to explain: time and space complexity for common operations, when to use it over alternatives, and at least two canonical problems it solves. Binary search trees, heaps (priority queues), and tries deserve their own focused sessions.

**Weeks 3–4: Algorithmic patterns.** Move from data structures to problem-solving patterns: two pointers, sliding window, BFS/DFS graph traversal, dynamic programming (memoization and tabulation), backtracking, and divide-and-conquer. The goal is recognizing which pattern applies to a new problem within the first 60 seconds of reading the prompt.

**Tracking:** Maintain a problem log — not just solved/unsolved, but the pattern you recognized, where you got stuck, and what you would do differently. Reviewing this log weekly compounds your learning faster than solving new problems blindly.

**Daily schedule suggestion:** 1.5–2 hours of problem-solving on weekdays; one longer session (3–4 hours) on weekends for harder problems and review. Do not skip rest days — cognitive consolidation happens during downtime.

## Month 2: System Design, Behavioral Prep, and Company Research

Once your algorithmic foundation is solid, broaden your preparation to the other two major interview dimensions.

**System design (weeks 5–6).** Work through canonical design problems: URL shortener, key-value store, distributed message queue, news feed, ride-sharing dispatch. The goal is not memorizing solutions but developing a reusable framework — requirements clarification, capacity estimation, high-level components, data modeling, deep-dive on the most interesting tradeoffs, and failure modes. Study consistency models (strong, eventual, causal), CAP theorem, replication strategies, and caching layers. Read the engineering blogs of companies you are targeting — real architecture decisions are more instructive than textbook examples.

**Behavioral preparation (weeks 6–7).** Assemble a library of 8–12 strong stories from your career using the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Cover: a time you navigated technical ambiguity, a conflict with a colleague or stakeholder you resolved constructively, a project where you drove impact beyond your role, and a significant failure with genuine lessons learned. Practice telling each story aloud — the difference between a written story and a fluent verbal delivery is substantial. Time yourself; most behavioral answers should land in 90–150 seconds.

**Company research (weeks 7–8).** For each target company, research: its core products and revenue model, recent engineering blog posts or conference talks, the team/org structure of the role you are targeting, and first-hand interview reports on Glassdoor, Blind, or Reddit. Understanding a company's actual technical stack and architectural philosophy helps you tailor system design discussions and signals genuine interest to interviewers.

**Tracking:** Create a simple spreadsheet with companies, roles, application status, interview stages, and notes from every conversation. Visibility into your pipeline prevents missed follow-ups and helps you calibrate timing across multiple processes.

## Month 3: Mock Interviews, Final Research, and Offer Negotiation

The final month shifts from learning to performance practice.

**Mock interviews (weeks 9–10).** Conduct at least 8–10 full mock interviews — 4–5 technical (coding + system design) and 4–5 behavioral. Use platforms like Pramp, interviewing.io, or arrange peer mocks with engineer friends. The simulation of being watched, timed, and expected to think aloud exposes habits (going silent, over-explaining, forgetting edge cases) that solo practice never reveals. After each mock, identify your top two improvement areas and focus the next session on those specifically.

**Final company-specific preparation (weeks 10–11).** As you receive interview invitations, do targeted preparation for each company's specific format. Some companies weight coding over system design; some include take-home projects; others emphasize product thinking. Adjust your final prep sessions to match the actual format. Re-read the job description and map your strongest stories to its stated requirements.

**Offer negotiation preparation (week 12).** Collect salary data from Levels.fyi, LinkedIn Salary, and your recruiter conversations before any offer arrives. Know your market value, your target total compensation, and your true walk-away point. Prepare to negotiate competing offers — even if you only have one, researching competing offers from similar companies creates legitimate leverage. Practice declining or countering an offer conversationally; most engineers find negotiation uncomfortable and benefit from role-playing it with a trusted friend.

## Staying Motivated Across 90 Days

The biggest risk to a 90-day prep timeline is losing motivation around weeks 4–6, when the novelty has worn off but the finish line is not yet in sight. A few tactics that help:

**Anchor to outcomes, not effort.** Track problems solved, mock interviews completed, and companies applied to — visible progress metrics create momentum. Effort hours alone feel like a treadmill.

**Build in recovery weeks.** Schedule one lighter week per month — reduce daily practice to 30 minutes of review only. This prevents burnout and actually consolidates earlier learning.

**Find an accountability partner.** A peer also preparing for interviews creates mutual accountability, someone to share interview reports with, and a source of encouragement when motivation dips.

The 90-day timeline is not a promise that preparation ends at day 90 — some processes run longer. But it is a realistic commitment that, if followed consistently, puts most engineers in a strong position to interview confidently at their target companies.
