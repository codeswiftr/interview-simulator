# How to Evaluate and Compare Job Offers

You've done the hard work — the LeetCode practice, the system design prep, the behavioral rounds. Now you have an offer (or two). The last thing you want is to make this decision poorly. This guide gives you a structured framework for evaluating software engineering offers beyond just the total compensation number.

## The Problem with Pure Comp Comparison

Salary comparison feels objective but is actually one of the most misleading ways to compare offers. A $200K base at a pre-revenue startup is fundamentally different from a $200K base at Apple. A $180K base in New York is different from the same number in Austin.

The better approach: evaluate offers across multiple dimensions, then decide what weight to assign each based on where you are in your career.

## Compensation: Full Picture

Start with total annual compensation, not base salary.

**Annualize everything:**
```
Year 1 total comp = Base salary
                  + Annual bonus (or expected bonus %)
                  + (Equity value / vesting period)
                  + Signing bonus (year 1 only)
                  - Income tax differential (if moving cities)
```

**Evaluate equity carefully:**

*Stock options* (common at startups): Only valuable if the company exits above the strike price. Value = (current valuation per share - strike price) × shares × expected dilution × probability of exit. This is speculative.

*RSUs* (common at public companies): Vest into actual shares. Value = shares × current stock price × vesting schedule. Real value, subject to market risk.

*Tax implications*: ISO options vs. NSO options vs. RSUs have very different tax treatments. For grants over $50K in estimated value, consult a tax advisor.

**Ask about equity terms:**
- What is the current 409A valuation (for options)?
- What is the vesting schedule (4-year with 1-year cliff is standard)?
- What are the post-termination exercise windows? (90 days is standard but brutal; 5–10 years is founder-friendly)
- Is there accelerated vesting on acquisition?

## Growth: Where Will This Take You?

The comp question is "how much will I earn?" The growth question is "who will I become?"

**Manager quality**: The most important factor in your professional development. Ask:
- Who would I directly report to?
- Can I speak with my potential manager informally before deciding?
- What is their track record of promoting people?
- How many of their previous reports have been promoted or gone on to strong careers?

**Scope and challenge**: Will you be solving genuinely hard problems or maintaining legacy code?
- What does the engineering roadmap look like for the next 18 months?
- What's the most technically interesting work this team has done recently?
- What's the current biggest engineering challenge?

**Promotion and leveling**: 
- What does the path from your level to the next level look like?
- How long does it typically take?
- What's the promotion rate on this team?

**Brand value**: Some companies are talent factories that make future career moves easier. Alumni from Stripe, Google, Airbnb, Plaid, and similar companies carry disproportionate credibility in the market.

## Company Health and Risk

Especially important if you're considering startups or pre-IPO companies:

**Runway and business model**:
- How much runway does the company have?
- Is revenue growing and at what rate?
- What is the path to profitability?

**Funding and investors**:
- Who are the investors? (Tier 1 VC backing matters for future funding rounds)
- When was the last funding round and at what valuation?
- Has there been a down round?

**Team stability**:
- What is the engineering attrition rate?
- How long have senior engineers been there?
- Have there been layoffs in the past 18 months?

You can research Glassdoor, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and PitchBook for this information.

## Culture and Work Environment

Culture is often the hardest to evaluate from the outside, but there are signals:

**Ask during interviews (and note the answers you get):**
- "Can you describe the last time something went significantly wrong? How was it handled?"
- "How do decisions get made on this team?"
- "How do you handle disagreement between engineering and product?"
- "What do you like most about working here? What would you change?"

**Signals from the interview process itself**:
- Were interviewers respectful of your time?
- Did they give you substantive feedback or engage with your questions genuinely?
- Did the process feel organized or chaotic?

**Work-life balance**:
- What are typical working hours?
- How are on-call responsibilities structured?
- Is there an expectation of availability on evenings/weekends?

## Location and Quality of Life

For in-person or hybrid roles:
- **Commute time**: A 1.5-hour daily commute is ~500 hours/year — that's 12 work weeks
- **Cost of living**: Use a reliable cost-of-living calculator; a $10K base salary difference can be irrelevant or decisive depending on the cities being compared
- **Remote work flexibility**: Even a hybrid role that requires 3 days/week in office has very different quality-of-life implications than 5 days

## Making the Decision

Once you've evaluated all dimensions, use a weighted scoring approach:
1. List your top 10 factors
2. Assign weights (they should sum to 100%)
3. Score each offer on each factor (1–5)
4. Calculate weighted totals

Then do a gut check: does the winner feel right? If not, examine which factors are driving the score and whether your weights reflected your actual values.

The offer evaluation process is also a negotiation window. Use it. Most engineers accept offers without negotiating, leaving real money on the table. If you have competing offers, use them as leverage. If you don't, you can still negotiate based on your market research.

Trust the process, but also trust yourself. The spreadsheet doesn't know which team you'll actually enjoy working with every day.
