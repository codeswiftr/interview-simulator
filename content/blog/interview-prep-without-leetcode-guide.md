# How to Get a Software Engineering Job Without Grinding LeetCode

The conventional wisdom about software engineering job searching says you need to spend months grinding LeetCode, memorizing graph traversal algorithms, and practicing dynamic programming on whiteboards. That advice isn't wrong for FAANG. But it's far from the only path — and for a large segment of the industry, it's not even the best one.

This guide is for engineers who want to target companies that evaluate you on real engineering skills: the ability to design systems, write maintainable code, work through ambiguous problems, and ship things that work.

## Companies That Don't Emphasize Algorithmic Puzzles

The interview process varies enormously by company type. Large consumer tech companies (Google, Meta, Amazon) have historically leaned heavily on LeetCode-style coding. But a substantial portion of the industry — especially growth-stage startups, infrastructure companies, and developer-tools companies — has moved away from this model, for a straightforward reason: it doesn't predict on-the-job performance particularly well.

**Stripe** is frequently cited as a model for skills-based interviewing. Their process has included systems design, code review of existing code (reading and improving real Stripe-style code rather than solving contrived puzzles), and conversations about technical tradeoffs. The signal they're looking for is how you think about real engineering problems, not whether you can implement a segment tree under time pressure.

**Shopify** uses take-home assignments and structured technical conversations. Candidates build something that resembles the work they'd actually do — a small API, a data processing pipeline, a component — then discuss it in depth. The interview is a conversation about your decisions, not a timed puzzle.

**Linear**, **Vercel**, **Loom**, and many developer-tools companies favor similar approaches: practical work samples, discussions of past projects, and systems design that focuses on the specific domain of the role. Targeting companies in developer tools, infrastructure, and B2B SaaS often means a process more correlated with actual engineering.

## Portfolio-Based Hiring and Open Source as Signal

Many engineers underestimate how much a strong public portfolio can shift the conversation. When a hiring manager can read your code before the interview, the interview starts from a different premise — they're evaluating you, not filtering you.

**Open source contributions** serve as verifiable evidence of your abilities. A meaningful contribution to a popular project — fixing a real bug, adding a documented feature, improving test coverage — demonstrates that you can navigate an unfamiliar codebase, communicate with maintainers through PR review, and produce code that meets community standards. This is closer to day-one job performance than most interview questions.

If you don't have open source contributions, targeted project work demonstrates similar signals. Build something in the domain of the company you're targeting: a CLI tool if you're applying to developer tools companies, a data pipeline if targeting data infrastructure, a small SaaS if applying to product-focused startups. The goal is to give interviewers something concrete to discuss rather than synthetic problems.

**Technical writing** — blog posts, documentation improvements, conference talks — also serves as portfolio signal. Engineers who write clearly about technical topics demonstrate the communication skills senior roles require and often attract inbound interest from companies who discover them through that content.

## Targeting Companies by Interview Process

Not all companies advertise their interview process upfront, but you can infer it. **Glassdoor and Blind** contain interview reports with enough specificity to understand what a process looks like. Startups in the Y Combinator network, Stripe alumni-founded companies, and companies in the developer tools space cluster toward practical interviews. Enterprise software companies and financial services firms vary more widely but often include systems design alongside coding.

**Ask directly** during the recruiter screen. "Can you tell me what the technical interview process looks like?" is a completely normal question, and the answer tells you how to prepare. If a company says "two LeetCode sessions and a systems design," you know what to practice. If they say "take-home project followed by a discussion," you know the energy is better spent on a clean, well-documented implementation.

Referrals matter especially in portfolio-based hiring — they put your work in front of a hiring manager with a warm introduction rather than requiring it to clear an automated keyword filter. Engineering communities, open source project maintainer relationships, and conference connections are all legitimate paths to referrals at companies whose interview processes you actually want to go through.

## Project-Based Interviews: How to Succeed

Take-home projects are often better opportunities than they feel. The pressure of a timed whiteboard is gone; you're evaluated on production-like code rather than puzzle-solving speed. But they require different preparation.

Treat take-home projects as you would a real work assignment: write a README, add tests, make deliberate design decisions you can defend, and leave the code in a state you'd be proud to show a senior engineer. The subsequent discussion is where the real evaluation happens — you should be able to explain every decision, acknowledge what you'd do differently with more time, and discuss how you'd extend the design.

The engineers who struggle with take-homes often underestimate the discussion phase. Read your own code before the interview as if you're reviewing a colleague's PR. Identify the parts you'd strengthen, think about the edge cases you didn't handle, and have a concrete answer to "what would you do with another day on this?"

Targeting companies that respect real engineering work, building a visible track record through open source and public projects, and investing in your ability to discuss engineering decisions clearly — these are the levers that matter in portfolio-based hiring. LeetCode is one path. It's not the only one.
