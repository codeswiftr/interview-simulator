# Conference Speaking for Developers: A First-Timer's Guide

Speaking at technical conferences seems intimidating from the outside—reserved for experienced practitioners with famous names. This is wrong. Most conference talks are given by working engineers who solved a specific problem and shared what they learned. You qualify.

## Why Engineers Underestimate Their Own Knowledge

The "curse of knowledge" is real: once you've learned something, it's hard to remember how hard it was to learn. Topics that feel obvious to you feel that way because you've internalized them. To someone earlier on the same path, your knowledge is genuinely valuable.

The most useful conference talks are not from industry legends speaking abstractly. They're from practitioners saying: "We had this specific problem. Here's exactly how we solved it. Here's what didn't work. Here's what we'd do differently."

You have these stories. You've shipped features, debugged production incidents, migrated databases, designed systems. Any of these is a talk.

## Finding Your Talk Ideas

Start with problems you've actually solved:
- "We migrated from microservices back to a monolith and here's everything that happened"
- "I built a feature that serves 10M requests/day and here's what I wish I'd known"
- "Here's how we reduced our build times from 45 minutes to 8 minutes"

Other productive sources:
- Things you explain to colleagues frequently (if you explain it repeatedly, others need it too)
- A failed approach and what you learned from it (failure talks are often the best-attended)
- A mental model or framework that changed how you think about something
- A technology you've used deeply that others are curious about

**Avoid**: "Introduction to X" talks where X has a dozen existing tutorials. Unless you have a genuinely unique angle, introductory talks compete with free resources and are hard to get accepted.

## Writing a Winning CFP Submission

The CFP (Call for Proposals) abstract is your sales pitch. It's read by a committee who will see hundreds of submissions. Structure for an effective abstract:

**The hook**: First sentence must capture the reader's attention. "Last year, our search service went down for 4 hours and we had no idea why." This is more compelling than "In this talk, we will discuss distributed tracing."

**The problem context**: One paragraph establishing the problem and why it matters to the audience.

**What the talk covers**: Concrete takeaways in bullet form ("Attendees will learn: how to set up distributed tracing with OpenTelemetry, the three places to instrument first, and how to query traces to find root causes in < 5 minutes").

**Speaker credibility**: One sentence: "I'm a senior engineer at [Company] who has been working with distributed systems for 6 years. Our tracing implementation now covers 200 services."

Write several drafts. Get feedback from colleagues before submitting.

## Targeting the Right Conferences

Start local and work up:

**Local meetups**: The lowest-stakes venue. 30-50 people. Informal. Perfect for a first attempt. Most meetups are actively looking for speakers—reach out to organizers.

**Regional conferences**: 200-500 attendees. More formal CFP process. Single or multi-track. 30-45 minute talks. Good middle step.

**Major conferences (KubeCon, PyCon, JSConf, StrangeLoop)**: Highly competitive CFPs. 1,000+ attendees. Industry visibility. Build toward these after some conference experience.

**Company developer conferences**: AWS re:Invent, Google Cloud Next, Microsoft Build — these want customer speakers who've built things on their platforms. High visibility, technically demanding audience.

**Online conferences**: Lower barrier, still valuable. Global audience. Often recorded.

## Preparing the Talk

**Structure**: Opening story → Problem → Solution → Lessons learned → Resources → Questions

**For 30-minute talks**: Aim for 4-6 main points. More than that rushes; fewer feels thin.

**Slide design**: Less text, more visuals. Diagrams over bullets. Code examples with syntax highlighting. One idea per slide. No walls of text.

**Code on slides**: Keep code snippets < 10 lines. Extract the essential part. Walk through it slowly—attendees can't read code and listen simultaneously.

**Practice out loud**: You cannot practice presentations in your head. Speak the words aloud. Record yourself. Watch it back. Do the full talk at least 3 times before the event.

**Rehearse transitions**: The hardest parts of a talk are often between sections. Practice the connective tissue: "So that's how we set up the instrumentation — now let's look at what the data actually tells us."

## The Day Of

**Arrive early**: Test your AV setup. Know where the connectors are. Bring your own dongles (HDMI, USB-C to HDMI, DisplayPort). Technical difficulties that could have been prevented in advance will rattle you.

**Handling nerves**: Some nervousness is normal and helpful. The adrenaline sharpens focus. Counterproductively, many engineers try to eliminate all nervousness—instead, channel it. Your nervousness signals to you that this matters.

**The beginning is hardest**: The first 2 minutes are the most nerve-wracking. Once you're moving, it gets easier. Know your opening cold — have it memorized so your brain can autopilot while your nerves settle.

**Taking questions**: "I don't know" is a complete answer. "That's a great question — I haven't encountered that case. I'd love to follow up with you after." Don't guess or make things up. Your credibility is on the line.

## Career Impact

Concrete career benefits of a conference speaking track record:

**Visibility**: The people in the room and those who watch the recording include potential future employers, collaborators, and career advocates.

**Credibility**: "I've spoken at PyCon about our caching strategy" establishes domain credibility in a way that's hard to fake.

**Network building**: Post-talk conversations are often the start of professional relationships. Other practitioners who share your specific problem find you.

**Company value**: Representing your company at a conference is visible to leadership. It signals technical depth and communication ability — both relevant to promotion conversations.

**Passive inbound**: A good conference talk gets views on YouTube for years. Engineers finding the same problem discover your talk, your blog, your profile.

## Starting Today

Don't wait until you have enough experience. Submit a talk to a local meetup this month. Use a problem you've actually solved. The craft of presentation improves with practice — the only way to get the practice is to start.

Your first talk will be imperfect. Give it anyway. The engineers who speak at major conferences got there by first speaking at small ones.
