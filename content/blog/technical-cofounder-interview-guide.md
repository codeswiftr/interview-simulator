# Technical Cofounder Interview Guide: Evaluating and Joining Early-Stage Startups

Joining a startup as a technical cofounder is one of the most significant career decisions an engineer can make. Unlike a standard engineering role, you're evaluating a long-term partnership, a market opportunity, and a life commitment simultaneously. This guide covers how to evaluate cofounder conversations and what both parties should assess.

## What Makes a Technical Cofounder Different from Employee #1

The distinction matters before you start any conversation:

**Cofounder**: Equity split (typically 10-50% depending on stage, role, and negotiation), explicit organizational title, strategic decision-making authority, legal documents (founder agreement, vesting schedule, IP assignment), and genuine co-ownership of the company direction. You are betting your career on this company.

**Early engineer (employee #1-5)**: Options grant (typically 0.5-2% at seed, 0.1-0.5% at Series A), standard employment relationship, execution role rather than strategic. More security but dramatically less upside.

If you're in a "cofounder" conversation, establish which of these you're actually being offered. "Co-founder" is sometimes used loosely to attract engineers who'd otherwise negotiate for higher engineer compensation.

## Evaluating the Founding Team

The team is the single most important variable at early stage:

**Complementary skills**: A great technical cofounder conversation with a solo non-technical founder is valuable — but probe whether they have real domain insight or are looking for a technical partner to validate their idea. The ideal combination is a founder with genuine domain expertise + a builder who can execute.

**Chemistry and conflict style**: You'll spend more time with your cofounders than your family during the early years. Disagreements are inevitable — how you navigate the first difficult conversation in your evaluation process predicts how you'll navigate future ones. Deliberately introduce a tension point to observe how they respond.

**Track record**: What have they built before? Successful exits are nice but not necessary — evidence of shipping things, learning from failure, and executing under uncertainty matters more than a single outcome.

**References**: Talk to people who've worked with them before. Ask: "Would you work with this person again? What's their working style under stress? What are their blind spots?" Reference calls with former colleagues reveal what LinkedIn profiles hide.

## Technical Due Diligence

As the technical cofounder candidate, do proper technical assessment of the opportunity:

**Market size and moat**: Is the market large enough to support a venture-backed outcome? If not, is there a self-funded path? What's the defensible moat — network effects, proprietary data, switching costs, regulatory advantage? Can you build something technically defensible?

**Technical feasibility**: What's the actual technical challenge? Is there genuine technical innovation required, or is this execution on known patterns? Both are valid, but they require different skills and attract different cofounder profiles.

**Existing code quality**: If there's a prototype, ask to review it. Not to judge harshly — early prototypes are always messy — but to understand the current technical debt, the existing tech choices, and whether the founding engineer (if different from you) thinks about software quality in compatible ways.

**Data and compliance requirements**: Regulated industries (healthcare, finance, legal) have compliance requirements that can significantly affect technical architecture and hiring. Understand what you're building into before committing.

## Equity and Legal Foundations

Get these right before signing anything:

**Equity split negotiation**: Common splits range from 50/50 (equal cofounders, both in from the start) to 80/20 or 70/30 (existing solo founder, bringing in technical cofounder later). Splits should reflect current contribution and future commitment, not past work alone. Negotiate the split clearly and document it.

**Vesting schedule**: Industry standard is 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff. This protects both parties — neither is fully vested immediately, and the cliff means you don't immediately owe equity to someone who leaves after 3 months. Negotiate acceleration provisions (single-trigger vs. double-trigger on acquisition).

**Intellectual property assignment**: All code you write for the company belongs to the company. Make sure this is documented. Also ensure your previous employer agreement doesn't create IP conflicts with the new venture.

**Founders' agreement**: Before taking salary or vesting equity, have a founders' agreement that covers: decision-making authority, what happens when a founder leaves, vesting terms, and buyout provisions. This conversation is uncomfortable before you've started but far more uncomfortable when there's real value at stake.

## Are You Ready for the Technical Cofounder Role?

Honest self-assessment matters:

**Can you build 0→1?**: Early-stage technical cofounder work is different from engineering at scale. You'll write all the code, make architecture decisions alone, do DevOps, and potentially interview engineers before you can afford to hire. Comfort with full-stack ambiguity is essential.

**Are you comfortable with uncertainty?**: Cofounders don't have a product manager telling them what to build. You'll need strong opinions about what the product should be — not just how to build it.

**Financial runway**: Startup equity is illiquid and likely worthless at early stages. Do you have personal runway to take reduced or no salary for 6-12+ months? Can your life circumstances handle the uncertainty?

**Why this company, why this team**: If you can't answer this clearly and compellingly, don't join. Cofounder commitment is multi-year — vague enthusiasm won't survive the inevitable difficult moments.

## What to Build Before the Decision

Before committing, propose a paid or unpaid trial sprint: "Let's work together on [specific problem] for 2-4 weeks before formalizing the relationship." This is standard practice and any serious founding team will welcome it. It's how you discover working style compatibility before either party makes an irreversible commitment.

The technical cofounder role is one of the highest-leverage positions in tech — and one of the highest-risk. Take the time to evaluate it with the rigor you'd apply to an architectural decision.
