# FAANG vs. Startup Interviews: What's Actually Different

If you've spent any time on tech Twitter or Blind, you've probably encountered the annual debate: are coding interviews broken? Is LeetCode dying? Do startups even care about algorithms anymore? The answer, as with most things in software, is "it depends" — and what it depends on is the fundamental difference in what FAANG companies and startups are actually trying to measure.

Understanding this difference doesn't just help you prepare smarter. It helps you decide which offer is worth chasing in the first place.

## What's Actually Being Evaluated (And Why It Differs)

FAANG interviews are designed to solve a calibration problem at scale. When Google hires thousands of engineers per year across dozens of teams, they need a standardized signal that travels across hiring committees, levels, and time zones without degrading. That's why the format converges on LeetCode-style algorithmic questions and structured system design: not because these problems map perfectly to day-to-day work, but because they produce consistent, comparable data points across a massive hiring funnel.

Startups are solving a completely different problem. A Series A company hiring its 12th engineer isn't worried about calibration across hiring committees — they need someone who can ship a payments integration next Tuesday and argue coherently about whether to use Postgres or DynamoDB. The evaluation is immediate and practical: can this person do the specific job we need done, and will they not drive the team insane?

This isn't a values difference so much as a structural one. Both approaches have failure modes. FAANG can hire brilliant algorithmic thinkers who struggle in ambiguity. Startups can hire people who are excellent at their current stack but can't adapt when requirements change.

## The Algorithm Focus Divide

FAANG companies — particularly Google, Meta, and Amazon — still rely heavily on LC-medium and LC-hard problems for a reason that the "LeetCode is dead" discourse tends to miss: the format isn't going away because it works well enough, and switching costs are enormous. Standardized problems mean interviewers can be trained consistently, rubrics can be applied reliably, and hiring committees can compare candidates interviewed by different people on different days.

That said, there's variance even within FAANG. Amazon leans harder on leadership principles during the coding portion than Google does. Meta tends to ask more graph and tree problems. Apple's bar varies more by team than the others.

Most Series A-C startups, on the other hand, have shifted to take-home projects or practical coding problems: build a small API, add a feature to a toy codebase, debug a realistic bug in a simplified version of their stack. This isn't a signal that they're easier — it's a signal that they're evaluating different things. A take-home that asks you to design a rate limiter from scratch in four hours is testing product thinking, code quality, and documentation habits more than it's testing whether you can implement a sliding window in 40 minutes under observation.

## System Design Calibration

FAANG system design interviews follow a fairly consistent template: establish requirements (functional and non-functional), sketch high-level components, dive deep on one or two areas (database schema, caching strategy, consistency model), and explicitly address NFRs like availability and latency. Interviewers are listening for how you navigate trade-offs and whether you're reasoning at the right level of abstraction for your target level.

Startup system design is more conversational and context-specific. The question is usually some version of "how would you build X for us?" — where X is something close to what they're actually building. The interviewer isn't following a rubric so much as they're assessing whether you think like an engineer they'd want to work with. They want to hear your assumptions surfaced out loud, your instinct for what matters now versus later, and your willingness to say "I'd want to validate this with data before over-engineering it."

The adaptation: for FAANG, practice the structured template until it's automatic. For startups, practice thinking out loud with genuine curiosity about the problem, not just demonstrating that you know what a CDN is.

## Behavioral Interview Differences

FAANG behavioral interviews are explicit about their structure. Amazon is the most formalized example — every behavioral question maps to a Leadership Principle, and interviewers are looking for specific STAR-structured evidence. Google's Googleyness and Meta's behavioral rounds operate similarly: there's a rubric, and you're being evaluated against it, even if the conversation feels casual.

Startup behavioral interviews tend to be much more fluid. What starts as "tell me about yourself" can become a 30-minute conversation about your philosophy on technical debt, or a debate about a product decision they're actively making. The evaluation criteria are often implicit rather than explicit — cultural fit, intellectual honesty, and communication style matter as much as the content of your answers.

The preparation difference is significant. For FAANG, you want six to eight strong STAR stories that can be mapped to multiple principles and behavioral categories. For startups, you want a clear, honest narrative about who you are as an engineer and what you care about — because that's what the conversation will actually surface.

## How to Choose When You Have Both Options

This is the real question most people avoid asking directly. FAANG offers compensation and career capital that's hard to match — the brand opens doors for years afterward, the compensation at senior levels is substantial, and the engineering infrastructure you work with is world-class. The cost is a more structured, slower-moving environment where your individual ownership of any given problem is limited.

Startups offer a different kind of value: the equity may or may not be worth anything, but the ownership is real, the decisions you make matter immediately, and the learning curve is steeper in ways that can accelerate your development significantly.

When evaluating both, ask questions that surface the real trade-off. At FAANG: how long does it typically take to go from ideation to production? How much of my time will be in meetings versus building? What does internal mobility look like? At startups: what's the runway, and what does the path to Series B look like? How are engineering decisions made? What did the last person in this role go on to do?

The best choice depends on where you are in your career and what you're optimizing for. Neither interview format is inherently better — they're measuring different things for different environments. The advantage goes to the candidate who understands that distinction before they walk into the room.
