# Navigating Tech Interviews as an Underrepresented Engineer

Technical interviews are hard for everyone. For engineers from underrepresented groups — women, Black and Latino engineers, LGBTQ+ engineers, engineers with disabilities — they carry additional weight: navigating potential bias, evaluating whether a company's DEI commitments are real, and deciding how much of your identity to bring into the room. This guide offers practical frameworks for all of it.

## Recognizing Bias Without Internalizing It

Research on technical interviews consistently shows that identical performance can be evaluated differently based on the candidate's perceived identity. This is a real phenomenon, not a rationalization. Knowing this matters because it allows you to separate "I performed poorly" from "the evaluation was flawed."

Signals that an interview may be affected by bias:
- The interviewer seems surprised when you solve a problem correctly, or expresses that surprise verbally
- You're asked to "prove" qualifications that weren't asked of others in the same loop (sometimes visible when comparing notes with other candidates)
- Questions about your background or fit drift into territory that isn't asked of other candidates
- The tone shifts noticeably when you mention certain background details

How to respond in the moment: stay focused on the technical work. You don't owe an interviewer a performance of discomfort. Complete the problem, note what happened, and factor it into your decision. You can raise it with a recruiter after, or simply decline the offer if you see it as a pattern.

What you should not do: assume every difficult interview was biased. Interviewers can be awkward with everyone. Evaluate patterns, not individual moments.

## Evaluating Company Culture Authentically

The gap between DEI marketing and actual workplace culture is wide at many companies. "We value diversity" in a careers page is meaningless. Here's how to assess the real thing.

**Ask specific questions, not general ones.** "How does the company support diversity?" invites a rehearsed answer. Better questions:

- "Can you tell me about a time someone on this team raised a concern about team dynamics or inclusion? How was it handled?"
- "What's the gender/ethnicity breakdown of engineering leadership in this org, not just company-wide?"
- "How has the ERG influenced actual company decisions? Can you give an example?"
- "Have you seen people from underrepresented backgrounds promoted to staff/principal level here? What did that path look like?"

**Pay attention to interview composition.** If you're a woman and your entire 5-person interview loop is men, that's a data point. Ask your recruiter if they can add a panel member who reflects your background — legitimate companies will accommodate this.

**Look up the data.** Companies file EEO-1 reports with the federal government. Some publish diversity reports voluntarily. Compare representation at individual contributor level vs. management vs. senior leadership. Attrition tells you more than hiring numbers.

**Talk to current and former employees.** LinkedIn, Blind, and Glassdoor all have candid perspectives. Look specifically for comments from people who share your identity. Pay more attention to recent reviews (2-3 years) than older ones — culture can shift.

## Assessing Psychological Safety in the Interview

Psychological safety — the ability to speak up, ask questions, and admit uncertainty without fear — is more predictive of team health than most other metrics. You can probe for it during the interview itself.

Notice: Does the interviewer make space for you to think out loud, or do they pressure you with silence or hints of impatience? When you ask a clarifying question, is the response collegial or slightly condescending? Does the interviewer share their own thinking and uncertainty, or do they perform omniscience?

Ask directly: "How does the team handle disagreement about technical decisions?" or "Can you describe what it's like when someone proposes an approach the team disagrees with?" Listen for specific stories. Vague answers ("we have healthy debates") are less credible than concrete examples.

## When and Whether to Mention Identity

This is deeply personal and there is no universally correct answer. Some considerations:

**You are never obligated to discuss your identity.** Interviews are not confessionals. Your qualifications are the point.

**Mentioning ERG involvement can be a signal-seeking move.** If you say "I was involved in the Women in Engineering ERG at my last company," you're creating an opening for the interviewer to reveal how they respond. Genuine interest and thoughtful follow-up questions are a positive signal. Discomfort or quick redirection is a data point.

**Cultural background can be a strength in product discussions.** If you have direct experience with a market or user population that the product serves, that's relevant context to share. Engineers from Lagos who've used Flutterwave, or engineers who grew up navigating two languages, bring knowledge that's genuinely valuable — and you can frame it that way.

## Organizations and Programs That Deliver

Not all DEI programs are equal. These organizations have track records:

**Code2040** (code2040.org): Fellows program for Black and Latino software engineers, places engineers at top-tier tech companies with mentorship and community. One of the most respected pipelines in the industry.

**Lesbians Who Tech + Allies**: Largest LGBTQ+ women tech community globally. Annual summit, job board, scholarships. Strong network effect in Bay Area and NYC tech.

**Out in Tech**: Community for LGBTQ+ people in tech. Job board and mentorship.

**AfroTech**: Annual conference and year-round community for Black technologists. Strong for networking and job searching at companies that actively recruit there.

**Rewriting the Code**: Virtual community for women in tech with mentorship, company partnerships, and community.

**NSBE (National Society of Black Engineers)**: Annual convention is one of the largest recruiting events for Black engineers in the US.

**Hiring managers who attend these events** are generally more committed than those who merely list them in a job posting. Ask your recruiter if the company sent a delegation to relevant conferences.

## The Compounding Decision

Every interview is a two-way evaluation. Your technical skills are one dimension; the company's culture is another. Underrepresented engineers often stay at companies where they're isolated longer than they should because leaving feels like admitting failure. It's not. Finding an environment where you can do your best work is the goal — that's a strategic decision, not a retreat.

The best technical career is one where you're challenged, recognized, and surrounded by people who want you to succeed. Hold out for that.
