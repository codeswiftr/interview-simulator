# Remote Work Interview Tips for Software Engineers

Remote engineering roles are different from in-office roles with video calls. Truly distributed-first companies have different engineering cultures, different ways of making decisions, different expectations for communication — and different things they evaluate in interviews. This guide covers how to interview well for remote roles and how to evaluate whether a remote company will actually be a good remote experience.

## What Distributed-First Companies Actually Evaluate

Companies that have built their engineering culture around distributed work (GitLab, Automattic, HashiCorp post-IBM, Basecamp, Doist, Zapier, Buffer) evaluate specific things that on-site companies don't:

**Async communication ability**: Can you communicate clearly in writing without real-time clarification? Distributed teams make decisions in Slack threads, RFC documents, GitHub discussions, and recorded Loom videos — not hallway conversations. Interviewers assess this indirectly (how clearly you communicate in async interview rounds) and directly ("tell me about a complex technical decision you drove in a distributed team").

**Autonomy and self-direction**: Remote teams can't rely on ambient accountability (sitting next to your manager, being seen as working). Interviewers want evidence that you set your own direction, unblock yourself, and deliver without hand-holding. Behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time you had to work on a project with minimal direction" or "How do you prioritize when you have three competing priorities and no manager to arbitrate?"

**Documentation culture**: Distributed-first companies write things down — architecture decisions, meeting notes, onboarding guides, incident postmortems. Interviewers often ask about your documentation habits. "I prefer to communicate in person" is a yellow flag for a distributed-first role.

**Time zone handling**: For globally distributed teams, time zone overlap management is a real skill. How do you make decisions when stakeholders are 9 hours apart? How do you handle incidents that happen outside your working hours? Strong candidates have specific practices: defined overlap windows, async-first protocols, documented escalation paths.

## How Distributed Interviews Differ

Remote interviews are often structured to reflect the working style:

**Written communication rounds**: Some distributed-first companies include a written exercise — a technical problem you solve asynchronously and write up, or a take-home project with a written summary. This is a direct test of async communication. Treat it as if you're writing a design doc for your future teammates: clear structure, explicit tradeoffs, appropriate brevity.

**Async video rounds**: Companies using Loom or similar tools for async interview questions expect you to communicate technical ideas to a camera without interruption or feedback. Practice talking through technical decisions without asking clarifying questions — distributed engineers default to stating assumptions and proceeding rather than blocking on clarification.

**The "work-style" interview**: Expect questions that are unusual in on-site interviews: "What does your home office setup look like?" "How do you structure your day?" "How do you handle Zoom fatigue?" These aren't small talk — they're evaluating whether you've thought about sustainable remote work.

## Questions to Ask Remote Employers

The quality of a remote role depends heavily on whether the company has actually invested in distributed infrastructure, or just moved their on-site culture to video calls. These questions reveal the difference:

"What's your default for synchronous vs. asynchronous communication?" — A distributed-first culture defaults to async; a converted-on-site culture defaults to meetings.

"How are technical decisions documented and made?" — Look for: RFC processes, architecture decision records, public Slack/GitHub discussions. Watch for: "we discuss it in the weekly meeting."

"What's the overlap policy for timezones?" — Some companies require specific overlap windows; others are fully async with no synchronous requirements. Know what works for you.

"How does onboarding work?" — Remote onboarding quality varies dramatically. Strong answer: structured onboarding program, assigned onboarding buddy, documented first 30/60/90 days. Weak answer: "you'll meet everyone and figure it out."

"What tools do you use for decision-making and institutional knowledge?" — Notion, Confluence, GitHub Discussions, Loom, or similar for persistent knowledge. If the answer is "Slack" primarily, knowledge is ephemeral.

## The Practical Setup Questions

Remote companies often assess your practical readiness:

**Equipment**: Some companies provide equipment (strong signal), others provide stipends ($500-2000 is typical), others expect you to use your own. Understand this before accepting.

**Internet requirements**: Some roles require specific upload/download speeds or backup connections for incident response. Ask if this is a requirement.

**Working hours flexibility**: True async companies offer significant time zone flexibility. Companies that call themselves remote but schedule 9am-5pm meetings in HQ time zone are effectively "remote in body, on-site in practice."

## Evaluating the Offer

Remote roles have different compensation structures. Some remote-first companies (GitLab publicly) pay based on local market rates (lower pay in lower cost-of-living areas). Others pay location-independent rates. Understand the policy before comparing offers — a $180K remote role in Austin may be equivalent to $160K from a company with location-adjusted pay.

The best remote companies have invested years in building distributed culture. You can usually tell within two interviews whether a company has that investment or is making it up as they go.
