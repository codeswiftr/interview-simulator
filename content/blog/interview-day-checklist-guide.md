# Interview Day Checklist: Technical Setup, Mental Prep, and Logistics

Most interview prep focuses on what to study. This guide focuses on the day itself — the logistical and mental preparation that determines how well you perform with the skills you've built.

## 48 Hours Before

**Confirm all logistics.** Check your calendar invite for: exact time (with time zone), whether it's phone/video/onsite, meeting link or address, and your interviewer's name. Ambiguity here causes unnecessary stress.

**Test your technical setup.** If it's a video call: test audio, video, and screen sharing. If you'll be coding in a shared environment: test CoderPad, HackerRank, or whatever platform they use. Know how to compile and run code in it. If it's onsite: know the address, transit time, and whether you need ID to enter the building.

**Set up your coding environment.** Have your preferred editor theme (light or dark, whichever reduces fatigue), your preferred language syntactically fresh in your mind, and your font size readable on screen. Small environmental friction accumulates into mental load on interview day.

**Prepare your questions.** Have 5-8 genuine questions about the role, team, and company. You won't use all of them — but running out of questions mid-interview is awkward and looks disengaged.

## The Night Before

**Do a light review, not a deep grind.** Reviewing familiar patterns is calming and reinforces retention. Starting new material the night before is stressful and rarely pays off.

**Prepare what you're wearing.** For remote: whatever makes you feel professional. For onsite: check the company's dress code culture (engineering offices are usually business casual or casual — "startup casual" is fine at most tech companies). When in doubt, one notch more formal is safer than one notch less.

**Set an alarm with margin.** For remote: 30 minutes before to handle technical issues. For onsite: 45-60 minutes of transit margin. Being early has no cost. Being late has a significant one.

**Get enough sleep.** Decision fatigue is real. Solving algorithm problems while sleep-deprived is significantly harder. A well-rested mediocre prep session beats an exhausted perfect-knowledge session.

## The Morning Of

**Eat a real meal.** Blood sugar impacts cognition. Don't interview hungry. Avoid anything that causes energy crashes.

**Do a brief warmup.** Solve one easy LeetCode problem you've done before — something that reminds your brain that you can do this. Don't start a new hard problem.

**Review your STAR stories.** Read through your behavioral stories to bring them to top of mind. You'll answer more naturally when the stories are fresh.

**Logistics run.** For remote: check your internet connection, mute notifications (Slack, email, phone), close unnecessary browser tabs, find a quiet room. For onsite: leave early enough that you're calm when you arrive.

## During the Interview

**First 2 minutes.** Introduce yourself. If asked "tell me about yourself" — have a 90-second version prepared: current role, what you've worked on recently, what you're looking for. Don't ramble for 5 minutes.

**If you don't understand a question.** Ask for clarification. "Could you say more about what you mean by X?" or "Could you give me an example?" shows engagement, not weakness. Answering the wrong question confidently is worse.

**If you need time to think.** "Give me a moment to think about this" is fine. Better: "I'm thinking about [approach A] versus [approach B] and trying to figure out which makes more sense here." Shows active thinking, not deer-in-headlights.

**Keep track of time.** Glance at the clock occasionally. If you're 20 minutes into a 45-minute coding session and haven't started coding, you need to converge on an approach.

**At the end.** Ask 2-3 of your prepared questions. "What's the biggest challenge the team is working through right now?" is a strong one. Thank your interviewers by name if you remember them.

## After the Interview

**Write your notes immediately.** In the first 10 minutes: what questions were asked, what went well, what you'd answer differently, anything technical you want to research. Memory degrades fast.

**Send a thank-you email.** Within 24 hours. Brief: "Thanks for taking the time to talk with me — I enjoyed the conversation about [specific thing]. I'm excited about the opportunity." If you want to add a clarification on something you said poorly, this is the place. Keep it to one paragraph.

**Rate your performance honestly.** On a 1-5 scale for each round: coding, system design, behavioral. Be accurate, not generous. This calibration informs your prep for remaining interviews at this company and future ones.

**If you bombed a round.** It happens. You might still advance — interviewers weigh rounds differently, and one weak round doesn't always sink a candidacy. But if you know a round went poorly, use the remaining interviews to compensate: strong behavioral stories can offset a weak system design, and vice versa.

## The Wait

Timelines vary wildly. FAANG might take 2-4 weeks from on-site to offer. Startups can move in days. Don't interpret silence as rejection. One week after an on-site: following up with your recruiter is appropriate. "I wanted to check in on the timeline for a decision — is there anything additional you need from me?"

While you wait: continue interviewing elsewhere. Having competing processes in progress reduces anxiety and improves your negotiating position if you receive an offer.
