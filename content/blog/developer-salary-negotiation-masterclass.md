# Developer Salary Negotiation Masterclass

Most engineers leave significant money on the table when accepting job offers. The gap between the first offer and what's actually available is often 10-30% — sometimes more. This guide gives you the tools, scripts, and strategies to negotiate effectively without damaging relationships.

## The Foundational Principle: Negotiation is Expected

Companies build negotiation room into initial offers. This is industry standard, not a secret. Recruiters expect candidates to negotiate. "Taking the first offer" is unusual behavior from their perspective.

Negotiating does not:
- Make you seem greedy
- Put the offer at risk (legitimate offers are not rescinded because a candidate asked for more)
- Hurt your relationship with the team before you start

Not negotiating does:
- Leave money on the table
- Signal that you accept the first number, which anchors future raises and negotiations

## Understanding Total Compensation

Before negotiating, understand what you're negotiating:

**Base salary**: Straightforward cash. The foundation of your comp package.

**Equity (RSUs/Options)**: At public companies, RSUs (restricted stock units) vest over 4 years with a 1-year cliff. Value depends on stock price. At private companies, options are riskier and harder to value.

**Annual bonus**: Target bonus percentage × base salary. Not guaranteed — typically subject to company and individual performance.

**Signing bonus**: One-time payment on joining. Often used to compensate for unvested equity you're leaving behind.

**Benefits**: Health insurance quality varies enormously between companies. 401k match, HSA contributions, and parental leave are real money. Factor them in.

At big tech companies, total comp can be 2-4x base salary. For a $200K base role at a public company: $200K base + $100K/year RSUs + $40K bonus = $340K total comp.

**The negotiation insight**: All components are negotiable, not just base. A company that "can't move on base" might add $20K to signing, increase the initial RSU grant, or reduce the cliff.

## Research: The Power of Information

Go into every negotiation with data:

**Levels.fyi**: The best source for total compensation at tech companies, broken down by level and location. Use it before every negotiation with a public tech company.

**Glassdoor / LinkedIn Salary**: Less precise but useful for companies not on Levels.fyi.

**Blind**: Anonymous forum where employees discuss compensation. Often has current, specific data.

**Competing offers**: The strongest negotiation data is a competing offer. We'll cover this extensively below.

Know the range for your level at this company before you have the conversation.

## The Negotiation Script

When you receive an offer, don't respond immediately. Say: "Thank you so much — I'm very excited about this opportunity. Can I have until [2-3 business days from now] to review the full package?"

This buys you time to research and prepare.

When you call back:

**Express genuine enthusiasm first**: "I'm really excited about this role. I've been impressed by [specific thing about the team/product]. I'm hoping we can work through the compensation to make this work."

**Make your ask**: "Based on my research into compensation for this level at [company/market], and given [specific reason: competing offer / specific skill / experience], I was hoping we could get the base salary to [target number]. Is that possible?"

**Wait for their response**: Don't fill the silence. Let them respond.

**Key principles**:
- Name a specific number, don't give a range (ranges anchor to the bottom)
- Ask for 10-20% more than the offer — this leaves room to land 5-10% higher
- Give a brief reason for your ask (competing offer, research, specific value you bring) — not a list of reasons

## The Competing Offer Strategy

A competing offer is the most powerful negotiation tool available. How to use it:

**Acquire a legitimate competing offer**: Interview at 2-3 companies simultaneously. Even one offer gives you leverage.

**Disclose it appropriately**: "I have an offer from [Company B] at $X total compensation. [Company A] is my first choice, but I need to see the packages be comparable."

**Be truthful**: Claiming a fake competing offer is ethically wrong and tactically risky (companies verify). The strategy only works with real offers.

**Let the company respond**: They will usually ask to match or come close. You then decide genuinely based on fit, not just comp.

## Negotiating Equity

RSUs and options are often more negotiable than base salary. Questions to ask:

"What is the current 409A valuation?" (private companies — tells you if options are underwater)

"What's the vesting schedule?" — 4-year monthly vesting after 1-year cliff is standard; some companies offer accelerated vesting.

"Can the initial grant be increased?" — RSU grants are commonly increased for strong candidates.

"Is there a refresh schedule?" — Annual refresh grants after year 1 are common at tech companies.

At Series B+ startups, equity is often the most negotiable element. Founders are protective of cash; equity grants can sometimes be doubled.

## What You Can Negotiate Beyond Base

When "base salary can't move":

- **Signing bonus**: Often easier to move because it's a one-time cost (not ongoing headcount budget)
- **First-year review date**: "Instead of 12 months, can we do a 6-month performance check-in with potential salary adjustment?"
- **Additional PTO**: 5 extra days/year
- **Remote work flexibility**: Formally documented, not verbal
- **Title**: More relevant for compensation in future negotiations than current day
- **Start date**: If you have unvested equity at your current company, negotiate a start date that allows more to vest

## The Most Common Mistakes

**Giving a number first when asked**: "What are your salary expectations?" — "I'd like to learn more about the role before discussing compensation" or "I'm looking at market rate for this level, which I'd love to discuss after learning more." Don't anchor against yourself.

**Accepting verbal offers**: "We'll get you the offer letter next week" → You cannot negotiate what you haven't seen in writing. Wait for the written offer before negotiating.

**Making the negotiation adversarial**: This is a business conversation, not a battle. Be warm and enthusiastic about the role throughout. The tone is "I want this to work; I just need help getting the comp there."

**Negotiating once and stopping**: After they counter, you can counter again. One round is not the limit.

**Missing the deadline for competing offers**: Offers expire. If you have two competing offers and need both active simultaneously, be transparent about timelines with both companies.

## What Happens After You Start

Negotiation doesn't end when you join. Annual compensation discussions:

- Document your accomplishments quarterly (for your own records)
- Know your market value continuously (be passively active on the market)
- Ask for promotion and salary reviews explicitly — they rarely happen automatically
- Don't wait for your manager to bring it up; prepare a case and request the conversation

Every engineer should know what their skills would command in the current market. That knowledge—even if you never act on it—gives you confidence in every internal compensation conversation.
