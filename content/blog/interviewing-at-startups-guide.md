# Interviewing at Startups Guide

Startup interviews are fundamentally different from big tech interviews — different evaluation criteria, different formats, and a two-way due diligence process where you're assessing the company as much as they're assessing you. Engineers who approach startup interviews with the same preparation strategy as FAANG interviews often miss what actually matters at early-stage companies.

## How Startup Interviews Differ

**Practical over algorithmic**: Most startups don't use LeetCode-style algorithm puzzles. They evaluate whether you can build the things they need to build. Typical formats: take-home projects (build a small feature or API in 3-5 hours), live coding on realistic problems (debug this failing test, add an endpoint to this existing service), or technical discussions about system design for their specific domain.

**Pace and ownership signals**: Startups are hiring for people who can move fast and take ownership of problems without hand-holding. Interviews probe: Do you ask the right clarifying questions without excessive hand-holding? Can you make reasonable decisions with incomplete information? Do you take initiative in the interview itself?

**Culture and mission alignment**: At a 15-person startup, a single hire can materially change the team culture. Interviewers are evaluating fit more holistically — not just technical skill but whether you'll thrive in ambiguity, whether you're genuinely excited by the problem they're solving, and whether you'll contribute to a constructive culture.

**Speed**: Startup hiring processes are typically faster than big tech — 1-2 weeks from first contact to offer rather than 4-8 weeks. Some companies make offers in days. If a startup's hiring process takes 3 months, that's actually a signal about how they operate.

## Due Diligence: Evaluating the Startup

Joining a startup is a more significant bet than joining a large company. Your due diligence matters:

**Runway and funding**: How much money has the company raised? What was the last round's valuation? When did they last raise? Runway (typically 18-24 months is healthy; less than 12 months is a yellow flag) determines whether the company will be around for your equity to vest. Ask directly: "How much runway do you have at current burn?" Founders who won't answer this question clearly are a red flag.

**Revenue and growth**: Is the company revenue-generating? What is the growth rate? For pre-revenue companies, what is the key metric and how is it trending? Strong growth suggests product-market fit; flat growth warrants scrutiny.

**Founding team quality**: Have the founders done this before? What is their relevant domain expertise? Are they credible builders? A first-time founder in a new domain is a harder bet than a second-time founder in their area of expertise.

**Engineering team health**: Is there technical debt so severe that the team spends most time fighting fires? What is the test coverage situation? How is code reviewed? Is there documentation? These aren't disqualifying at early stage, but understanding the state of the codebase tells you what you're walking into.

**Why is this role open?**: If someone left, why? If it's a new position, why now? "The previous person found the role wasn't right for them" can mean many things.

## Equity: What to Understand and Negotiate

**Option grants at startups**: Most early-stage startups grant ISO (incentive stock options) rather than RSUs. Options have a strike price set at the 409A valuation at the time of grant. You must exercise them to own shares — which requires paying the strike price and potentially triggering a tax event.

**Strike price and dilution**: The strike price should be below the latest preferred share price (usually the last funding round price). Understand how much dilution has occurred and will occur. An option for 0.5% of a company raises and dilutes through series A, B, C — your percentage shrinks. The terminal value matters more than the percentage.

**Typical equity ranges**: First 10 employees: 0.5-1%+. Employees 11-50: 0.1-0.5%. Employees 50-100: 0.05-0.1%. Series B+ senior engineers: 0.05-0.2%. These are rough guides — use them to assess if an offer is reasonable.

**Vesting**: Standard 4-year with 1-year cliff. Negotiate double-trigger acceleration (if the company is acquired AND you're terminated, your unvested options accelerate).

**90-day exercise window**: After leaving, options typically expire in 90 days — often forcing a buy-or-lose decision. Some companies (particularly post-Series B) offer extended exercise windows (5 years or more). Ask about this; it significantly changes the option value for employees who might leave before a liquidity event.

## Positioning Yourself for Startup Roles

**Show builder instincts**: Startups want engineers who want to build products, not just write code. Connect your interest in the company to the specific problem they're solving. "I've actually experienced this problem as a user" or "I've worked in this domain and understand the core challenge" resonates.

**Demonstrate velocity**: Examples of shipping quickly, adapting to change, and operating with autonomy are exactly what startup interviewers are listening for. "I built and deployed X in two weeks" beats "I contributed to a year-long platform migration."

**Be honest about what you want**: If you want startup experience for the learning, the ownership, and the equity upside — say so. Startups appreciate engineers who have thought clearly about the tradeoff they're making, rather than those who are joining because they couldn't get a FAANG offer.
