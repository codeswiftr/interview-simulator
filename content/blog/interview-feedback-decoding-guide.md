# Decoding Interview Feedback: What Recruiters and Interviewers Actually Mean

Interview feedback is frequently vague, sanitized by legal review, or delivered through a recruiter who wasn't in the room. Learning to decode what's actually being communicated helps you extract useful signal from otherwise opaque responses.

## Why Feedback Is Vague

Companies are cautious about detailed rejection feedback for legal reasons — specific feedback can be interpreted as documenting discrimination claims. Recruiters often can't share the actual interviewer comments ("too junior," "weak system design") and instead deliver softened summaries.

This means the feedback you receive is almost always less specific than what was actually said in debrief. Knowing this, you should treat any specific feedback as a high-confidence signal worth acting on, and treat vague feedback as a starting point for your own reflection.

## Common Feedback Phrases Decoded

**"We decided to move forward with candidates whose experience more closely aligns."**
Translation: You were technically assessable but someone else was a better fit on paper — usually more experience, a specific technology, or a domain match. This is often about the job description's requirements, not your interview performance. Ask the recruiter: "Was this a performance issue or a profile match issue?"

**"We're looking for someone with stronger technical fundamentals."**
Translation: A specific technical area was weak. Common culprits: system design for senior roles, algorithm depth for technical screens, depth vs. breadth in your answers. This is actionable. Identify which round and which type of question, and study that area specifically.

**"Culture fit wasn't quite right."**
Translation: This phrase covers a wide range and is the hardest to decode. It can mean:
- Communication style mismatch (too verbose/terse, talking over the interviewer)
- Disagreement with the interviewer that wasn't handled constructively
- Energy/enthusiasm read as low or forced
- Genuinely value-based (work style, collaboration preferences)

Ask the recruiter to be specific: "Can you share any concrete examples of what came up?" If they can't, you may need to do self-assessment or ask a peer who's observed you interview.

**"We thought you were strong but went with someone more senior."**
Translation: This is often true. It means you cleared the bar but were the second-choice candidate. You may be close to the next level and a few more months of experience would clear the gap. It's worth asking: "Would I be considered for future roles or at a more junior level?"

**"We'd encourage you to reapply in 6-12 months."**
Translation: You were close. Either specific gaps they think you can address, or a process issue (wrong role level). The specific timeframe suggests they're serious — companies that are being polite usually say "we'll keep your resume on file" without a specific date.

## How to Request Better Feedback

Most rejection emails don't invite feedback requests, but asking directly often works:

"Thank you for letting me know. I'd appreciate any specific feedback that could help me improve — even one area where I fell short would be very helpful. I understand if that's not possible to share, but I wanted to ask."

About 30-40% of the time, this generates a more specific response. Timing matters: ask within 24 hours of the rejection, while the recruiter is still engaged with your process.

For recruiters you've built a relationship with: a phone call is more effective than email. Spoken feedback is less filtered than written.

## Reading Signals During the Interview

You don't have to wait for post-interview feedback. Read the room:

**Strong signals:** Interviewers asking follow-up questions (they're engaged, not just checking boxes), describing what the role would actually look like in concrete terms, mentioning timeline and next steps unprompted.

**Weak signals:** Interviewer wrapping up significantly early without follow-up questions, generic "thanks, we'll be in touch," interviewer seeming distracted or checking the time frequently.

**Mixed:** Hints like "we've had trouble hiring for this role" (could be calibration issue, not about you) or "we have several rounds left to complete" (they're being thorough, not signaling).

## Using Feedback Across Rejections

If you're seeing patterns across multiple rejections — always failing the system design round, consistently getting "technical fundamentals" feedback, regularly not advancing past the final round — treat the pattern as a clear diagnostic:

- Same round failing → study that specific interview type
- Getting offers but rejecting or being rejected on comp → calibrate your expectations or improve negotiation
- Advancing but always losing to another candidate → you may be in a competitive pool; broaden your pipeline

Keep a simple log: company, role, which round you reached, feedback received (or best guess). After 5-10 interviews, patterns become clear. The engineers who improve fastest between interview cycles are the ones who treat their rejections as data, not as judgments of their worth.
