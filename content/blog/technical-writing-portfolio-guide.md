# Building a Technical Writing Portfolio for Engineering Roles

Technical writing is one of the highest-leverage skills a senior engineer can develop, and it's increasingly evaluated explicitly during the interview process. Design doc reviews, RFC feedback, and written engineering assessments have become standard at companies that care about engineering craft. A strong technical writing portfolio can differentiate you at the senior and staff level more effectively than any additional LeetCode preparation.

This guide covers what a useful portfolio contains, how to build one when you're starting from nothing, and what interviewers are actually looking for when they read your work.

## What Belongs in a Technical Writing Portfolio

The goal is to demonstrate that you can communicate technical ideas clearly to different audiences at different levels of detail. The most valuable artifacts are:

**Design documents and RFCs.** These are the highest-signal writing samples for engineers. A well-structured design doc shows that you can frame a problem, evaluate multiple approaches, articulate tradeoffs, and build consensus. Even a two-page design doc for a relatively minor system change demonstrates more about your engineering thinking than most interview answers.

**Runbooks and operational documentation.** Runbooks reveal whether you think about operational realities — failure modes, recovery steps, on-call decision trees. They also show whether you write for a reader under stress who needs to find information quickly. This is a distinct skill from design writing.

**Post-mortems and incident reviews.** A well-written post-mortem demonstrates systems thinking, intellectual honesty, and the ability to communicate failure without finger-pointing. If you have access to anonymized or public post-mortems you've authored, these are valuable samples.

**Blog posts or technical articles.** Public blog posts demonstrate your ability to write for a broader audience — explaining a technical concept, a debugging experience, or an architectural decision in a way that's accessible. These are lower-stakes to create and easier to share publicly.

**Architecture diagrams with written narratives.** A diagram alone is not technical writing. A diagram accompanied by written context — what the system does, why it's structured this way, what the key constraints were — is substantially more useful.

## Building Samples When You Have None

Most engineers have written more than they realize. Start by auditing what already exists:

- Slack threads where you explained a technical decision to a team
- Pull request descriptions that explained the context and approach, not just the diff
- Comments in design review discussions
- Team wikis or Confluence pages you authored
- Internal architecture notes written informally

These can often be refined and expanded into proper writing samples. A three-paragraph Slack message about why you chose Redis over Memcached for a specific use case can become a short but well-structured design note.

If you genuinely have nothing, create samples retroactively. Pick a system you've built or worked on and write the design document that should have existed. Write a runbook for a service you operate. Write a post-mortem for an incident that happened, even if a public one was never published. These retrospective documents are completely legitimate portfolio pieces — what matters is the quality of thinking they demonstrate.

Publishing on a personal blog (GitHub Pages, Substack, or a simple static site) gives you shareable URLs and a searchable archive. Several engineers have gotten interviews directly from blog posts that appeared in technical discussions.

## What Interviewers Are Actually Scoring

When an interviewer reviews your writing samples, they're evaluating several things simultaneously:

**Clarity.** Can you express a technical concept without ambiguity? Do readers need to reread sentences to understand what you meant? Clear technical writing is almost always short sentences, precise terminology, and explicit rather than implicit connections between ideas.

**Structure.** Does the document follow a logical progression? Strong technical documents have a clear problem statement before solutions, context before detail, and conclusions that follow from the evidence presented. Readers should be able to skim the headers and understand the arc of the document before reading it in full.

**Audience awareness.** The same technical decision needs to be explained differently to a junior engineer, a senior engineer, and an engineering manager. Interviewers look for whether your writing is calibrated to its intended reader — not too basic, not unnecessarily dense.

**Intellectual honesty.** Good technical documents acknowledge uncertainty, name the assumptions being made, and articulate the risks of the chosen approach. Documents that present a solution as obviously correct without acknowledging alternatives read as overconfident and are a red flag at senior levels.

**Concision.** Length is not a proxy for thoroughness. Interviewers read a lot of documents, and those that bury key information in excessive prose are harder to evaluate. The ability to be complete without being verbose is a distinct skill worth developing.

## Connecting Writing to Promotions

Technical writing is not just an interview artifact — it is the mechanism by which senior engineers exercise leverage. Engineers who write clear design docs get alignment faster. Engineers who write good runbooks reduce on-call burden for the whole team. Engineers who write post-mortems that generate actual systemic improvements build credibility across the organization.

Many promotion packets at senior and staff levels require written evidence of technical leadership. A pattern of strong design docs, RFCs, and written architectural decisions is often more compelling evidence of staff-level impact than the code itself.

Start building the habit now. Write a short design note for the next non-trivial decision you make. Publish a post about a debugging session that taught you something. Improve one runbook that currently exists only in someone's head. The portfolio compounds over time, and it pays dividends in interviews and in your actual job.
