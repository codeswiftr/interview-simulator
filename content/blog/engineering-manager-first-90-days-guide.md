# Engineering Manager First 90 Days: From Individual Contributor to Manager

The transition from individual contributor to engineering manager is one of the hardest in tech. Your measure of success inverts overnight. Code shipped, PRs merged, problems solved — the things that made you great as an IC are no longer your job. Your job is now to make everyone else great. The first 90 days determine whether you'll make that shift or silently cling to the identity that got you promoted.

## Letting Go of Code

The most common failure mode for new engineering managers is continuing to be a significant individual contributor. It feels productive, it's comfortable, and your team might even appreciate it in the short term. But it creates three serious problems:

**You can't manage what you're building.** The objectivity required to evaluate your team's technical decisions erodes when you're emotionally invested in a specific implementation. You'll unconsciously bias toward your own approach.

**You crowd out your team's growth.** Every interesting problem you claim is a learning opportunity you've removed from someone who needs it. Effective managers make work available, not scarce.

**You fall behind on people work.** 1-on-1s, career conversations, hiring pipelines, cross-team alignment — this work expands to fill the time you make for it. If you're coding all afternoon, the people work doesn't get done.

The practical fix: in your first 30 days, commit to writing zero production code. Contribute to design reviews, ask questions in code reviews, write documentation. But ship nothing. Use the discomfort of not coding as a diagnostic — if you feel idle without it, you haven't yet internalized what your new job is.

## 30/60/90 Day Framework

**Days 1-30: Listen and learn.**

Your primary task is to understand your team's current state before changing anything. Schedule a 1-on-1 with every direct report in week one. Ask: What's going well that you want to protect? What's broken that you'd fix if you could? What do you want to learn in the next year? What do you wish your manager knew?

Shadow your team's work. Attend standups, sprint reviews, retrospectives. Read recent design docs and post-mortems. Understand the current roadmap from the IC perspective, not just the PM's version.

Meet your key stakeholders: the PMs you work with, the EMs of adjacent teams, your skip-level. Ask each of them the same question: "What would a successful first six months look like from your perspective?"

Resist the urge to make changes. The one exception: if there's something broken that everyone already agrees is broken (a missing process, an unresolved conflict), fixing it quickly builds credibility. But anything controversial should wait.

**Days 31-60: Build trust and clarify expectations.**

Now you have enough context to start operating. Establish a clear 1-on-1 rhythm with each report — weekly for new or struggling team members, bi-weekly for strong senior engineers. Use these to understand their work, surface blockers, and build the relationship you'll need when harder conversations arise.

Clarify team operating agreements: how decisions get made, what needs your involvement, what you want engineers to own independently. Many teams inherited dysfunctional norms from previous managers. Naming them and proposing alternatives is a legitimate early move.

Have a direct career conversation with each report: Where do they want to be in two years? What does their ideal next project look like? What skills are they trying to develop? You can't advocate for someone's growth if you don't know what they're growing toward.

**Days 61-90: Drive outcomes.**

By day 60, you should understand your team's strengths, the technical landscape, and the stakeholder expectations. Now you can start making meaningful contributions to roadmap shaping, hiring decisions, and cross-team coordination.

Identify the one or two things your team most needs to improve — delivery predictability, technical debt, on-call burden — and start building a plan. This is also the time to have harder performance conversations if you've identified someone who is struggling.

## The Skip-Level Relationship

Your manager's manager has a stake in how you're doing. Establish a regular 1-on-1 early. Use it to get calibration on organizational context you don't have yet, to surface issues that require their involvement, and to ensure they have visibility into your team's work. Don't mistake skip-levels for a place to complain about your manager — it's a relationship-building exercise with someone who will have input on your career for years.

## Handling First Performance Issues

You'll likely have a direct report who isn't meeting the bar within your first 90 days. This is normal. The mistake new managers make is avoiding the conversation, hoping the performance issue resolves itself.

Start with curiosity, not judgment. "I've noticed the last few sprint commitments have slipped. What's been getting in the way?" Often the engineer already knows and is embarrassed. Sometimes there's a real blocker you can remove.

If the problem persists after a clear conversation, make expectations explicit in writing: "Over the next month, I need to see X, Y, Z. Let's check in every week." Document these conversations. Vague verbal feedback is not a performance improvement plan, and you'll need documentation if the situation escalates.

The first performance conversation you have as a manager is uncomfortable for everyone. Having it promptly and directly is a form of respect — the alternative is letting someone fail slowly in a job they're not performing in.
