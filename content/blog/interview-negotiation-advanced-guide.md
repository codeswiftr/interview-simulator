# Advanced Offer Negotiation: Competing Offers, Equity Deep Dives, and Closing

Most negotiation guides tell you to counter the first offer and justify it with market data. That's table stakes. The candidates who extract significantly better packages understand the levers beneath the surface: how to orchestrate competing offers, how to read equity structures, and how to close deals without leaving goodwill on the floor.

## The Competing Offer Playbook

A competing offer is the most powerful negotiation tool you have — but only if you use it correctly. Recruiters know the difference between a genuine offer and a bluff, and the downside of getting caught in one is severe.

**Timing competing offers:** The ideal scenario is having two or more offers simultaneously. If you're at the verbal offer stage with Company A and waiting on Company B, call your Company B recruiter: "I'm moving quickly with another opportunity and need to know your timeline. I'm very interested in your role specifically — is there any way to accelerate?" Most companies will expedite. A first-round interview can sometimes compress to five days when there's real urgency.

**Revealing competing numbers:** You don't need to show an offer letter. "I have a competing offer at $195K base plus equity" is sufficient. The recruiter's job is to close you, and they'll typically take your word for it. However, if a company asks for documentation before increasing their offer, you can share a redacted screenshot or simply restate the numbers confidently.

**Using the offer you actually don't want:** Even an offer from a company you'd never join is valuable. It anchors the negotiation and gives you information. "I have an offer from [Company X] for $180K. I'd prefer your company, but I need you to close the gap."

## Equity: RSUs vs Options Math

Equity compensation looks straightforward until you do the math and realize two packages advertised as "$200K total comp" are wildly different.

**RSUs (Restricted Stock Units):** RSUs vest into shares of stock you own outright. At public companies, calculate their value at current market price, then apply a haircut for the possibility the stock declines. A four-year grant of $400K in RSUs at $100/share (4,000 shares) is worth $400K today, but your actual take-home depends on the vesting schedule (typically 25% after year one, then monthly) and the stock price at each vesting date.

**Options (ISOs/NSOs):** Options give you the right to purchase shares at a strike price. The critical calculation is: (Current FMV - Strike Price) × Number of Options = Paper Gain. At early-stage companies, the strike price is often pennies but FMV is speculative. The real risk is exercising options at a significant cost only to see the company not exit. Always ask: What is the current 409A valuation? What was the last preferred share price? What's the liquidation preference stack?

**The signing bonus vs equity trade-off:** Companies use signing bonuses to replace unvested equity you're leaving behind. If you're leaving $50K in unvested RSUs at your current employer, ask for that in a signing bonus. However, signing bonuses are usually taxed as supplemental income (up to 37% federal), while equity at a public company is taxed at capital gains rates if held long enough. Model both scenarios before accepting one over the other.

## What's Actually Negotiable by Company Type

**FAANG/Big Tech:** Base salary has a band ceiling, but the band is often wider than the initial offer. Total comp is more elastic than base — they'll increase RSU grants before raising base. Signing bonuses are almost always negotiable. Level matters more than title; push to be leveled correctly before negotiating comp.

**Growth-stage startups (Series B-D):** Everything is negotiable but anchored by the option pool and hiring budget. Focus on strike price clarity, acceleration clauses (single-trigger vs double-trigger on acquisition), and the cliff on your vesting schedule. Ask about the company's last 409A valuation date.

**Early-stage startups:** Equity percentage matters more than grant size in shares. Negotiate for anti-dilution protection in writing if you're a founding team member.

## Scripts for Common Scenarios

**Countering the first offer:**
"Thank you for the offer. I'm very excited about the role. Based on my research and the competing opportunities I'm considering, I was expecting something closer to $X. Is there flexibility there?"

**When they say the offer is final:**
"I understand there may be constraints on the base. Is there flexibility in the signing bonus or the equity grant? I want to make this work."

**Closing on your terms:**
"If you can get to $X base and $Y in RSUs, I'm ready to sign today and decline the other offer. Can you make that happen?"

## Closing Without Burning Bridges

Always decline other companies in writing with gratitude. You'll interview again, and the recruiter you burned today is the hiring manager next year. "I've decided to pursue another opportunity, but I have a lot of respect for the team and hope we can stay in touch" costs you nothing and preserves a relationship.

Negotiation isn't adversarial — it's a preview of how you'll operate under pressure. Be direct, be honest about your constraints, and always leave the recruiter feeling like they won something too.
