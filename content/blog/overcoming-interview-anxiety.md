# How to Manage Interview Anxiety Without Pretending It Doesn't Exist

Here is something that interview prep guides rarely tell you: the senior engineer across the table from you, the one who looks completely relaxed, almost certainly experienced a spike of anxiety on the way in. Maybe they still do. Anxiety before high-stakes evaluation is not a weakness or a sign that you're underqualified. It is a normal physiological response, and pretending otherwise — or trying to suppress it — makes it worse.

What actually works is understanding what's happening in your body and mind, and applying techniques that work with those mechanisms rather than against them.

## Why "Just Calm Down" Is Terrible Advice

When you tell an anxious person to calm down, you are essentially asking them to override a system that evolved over millions of years to keep them alive. The anxiety response — elevated cortisol, increased heart rate, narrowed attention — is your threat-detection system doing its job. It doesn't know the difference between a job interview and a predator.

The physiological consequence most relevant to interviews is the effect on working memory. Cortisol and the related stress hormones impair the prefrontal cortex, which is exactly the brain region responsible for holding multiple pieces of information in mind simultaneously, suppressing irrelevant thoughts, and generating novel solutions. In other words, anxiety directly degrades the cognitive resources you need most in a technical interview.

This is why "just calm down" fails: suppressing anxiety requires cognitive effort, which further reduces the working memory you have available for the actual problem. You end up spending mental resources managing your anxiety rather than solving the problem, which increases anxiety because now you're behind, which consumes more resources — a textbook negative spiral.

## Evidence-Based Techniques That Actually Work

**Cognitive reframing: anxiety as excitement**

Research by Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard Business School found that people who said "I am excited" before a high-stakes performance performed measurably better than those who tried to calm themselves down. The physiological state of excitement is nearly identical to anxiety — elevated arousal, increased heart rate — but the cognitive framing changes how your brain interprets and uses that arousal.

The reframe is simple and it works because it's honest. You're not lying to yourself by saying you're excited — you genuinely do care about this opportunity, which is why the stakes feel high. Telling yourself "this activation I'm feeling means I'm ready" is more credible to your nervous system than "I'm not nervous at all."

**Controlled breathing with an extended exhale**

Box breathing and similar techniques are popular for good reason — they activate the parasympathetic nervous system (the "rest and digest" counterpart to "fight or flight") through a mechanism that's well understood. But the specific technique that produces the fastest anxiety reduction is an extended exhale.

Inhale for four counts, then exhale for six to eight counts. The extended exhale activates the vagus nerve directly, slowing heart rate within a few breaths. Do this for two to three minutes before you enter the building. It won't eliminate anxiety, but it will reduce arousal to a level where your prefrontal cortex is more accessible.

**Worst-case analysis**

Anxiety thrives on vague catastrophizing. Your brain generates a diffuse sense that "something terrible will happen" without ever specifying what, which means there's nothing concrete to refute. The fix is to be explicit.

Write down the actual worst case: you don't get the offer. Then write down what happens next. You apply to other companies. You learn from the debrief. Your life continues. When you force the catastrophe to be specific, it becomes survivable — and your threat-detection system starts to recognize that this situation doesn't actually warrant maximum alert.

## Building Anxiety Tolerance Through Deliberate Practice

All of the techniques above help manage anxiety in the moment. But the most durable solution is reducing your anxiety set point by accumulating interview experience in lower-stakes contexts.

Mock interviews are the most effective tool available, and the crucial distinction is that they must feel real enough to trigger mild anxiety. Reading about system design or doing LeetCode problems alone produces zero anxiety tolerance, because there's no social evaluation pressure. You need to actually speak your thinking aloud to another person who is evaluating you.

The mechanism here is exposure. Repeated exposure to mild versions of an anxiety-inducing situation, where you survive and perform adequately, teaches your threat-detection system that the situation is not dangerous. Over enough repetitions, the baseline arousal response diminishes.

Concretely: do at least one mock interview per week in the two months before you're actively interviewing. Use a partner, a practice platform, or record yourself on video. The recording option is underrated — watching yourself explain a solution activates a mild form of social evaluation anxiety even when you're alone.

## What to Do When Anxiety Peaks During a Live Interview

Despite preparation, anxiety will sometimes spike in the middle of an interview. You go blank on a problem you know, your mind empties, and silence starts to stretch. Here are specific scripts that buy you time and signal competence rather than panic.

"Let me think through this step by step — give me a moment to organize my approach." This is not stalling. It is what composed, senior engineers do. Interviewers respect it.

"I want to make sure I understand the problem correctly before I start coding. Is the input always sorted, or do I need to handle unsorted cases?" Asking clarifying questions serves two purposes: it gives you information you need, and it gives your working memory thirty seconds to recover.

"I'm seeing two possible approaches here. One is [X], which would be simpler to implement but has this trade-off. The other is [Y]. Which direction would be more useful to explore?" Presenting trade-offs is exactly what good engineers do, and it also gives you a moment to think while the interviewer responds.

If you go completely blank: "I know I've solved similar problems before. Let me start with the brute force approach to make sure I understand the structure, and then we can optimize." Starting from first principles — even with an inefficient solution — is almost always better than silence.

## The Post-Interview Debrief That Builds Resilience

Most candidates treat an interview as over the moment they walk out. The ones who improve fastest treat the debrief as its own discipline.

Within an hour of the interview, write down: what questions came up, how you answered them, what you'd answer differently, and what you noticed about your anxiety — when it peaked, what triggered it, whether any of your techniques helped.

Over time, this log becomes extremely valuable. You'll notice that the same scenarios trigger anxiety repeatedly, which gives you specific targets for your mock interview practice. You'll see evidence that you recovered from bad moments and still performed adequately — which is concrete data against catastrophizing. And you'll notice improvement, which is the best anxiety reducer of all.

Interview anxiety doesn't go away entirely, and you shouldn't want it to. A complete absence of arousal correlates with underperformance just as much as excessive anxiety does. The goal is not calm — it's optimal activation. That's a skill you can build deliberately, and it compounds over time.
