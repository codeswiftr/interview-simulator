Your resume has one job: survive the first 10 seconds.

That's not cynicism — it's reality. Recruiters at mid-to-large companies review hundreds of applications per open role. The initial screen isn't a deep read. It's a rapid triage. Your goal isn't to tell your full story in those first 10 seconds. Your goal is to not get rejected.

Everything else flows from that.

## What Recruiters Actually Look at in 10 Seconds

When a recruiter opens your resume for the first time, they scan in roughly this order:

1. **Company names** — Have you worked somewhere recognizable? Past employers act as a proxy for experience quality. Faang, known startups, or strong mid-tier companies all signal something. Unknown companies don't disqualify you, but they mean your bullets have to carry more weight.
2. **Job titles** — Are they progressing? Engineer → Senior Engineer → Staff Engineer reads well. Five years as "Junior Developer" at one company raises questions.
3. **Graduation year and school** — This tells them how many years of experience to expect and cross-checks it against your work history. Inconsistencies get noticed.
4. **Tech stack keywords** — Not a deep read, just a scan. Does this person know the languages and tools relevant to this role?

That's the 10-second screen. If anything in that scan raises a red flag or shows nothing interesting, the resume goes in the no pile. If it clears, they read more carefully.

## One Page or Two?

Under 10 years of experience: one page, no exceptions. Trim aggressively. If you're padding to fill two pages, that's obvious and it works against you.

Senior or staff level with genuinely dense experience: two pages is acceptable. Not required — many strong senior engineers keep it to one. But two pages is defensible if every bullet is earning its space.

Three pages: never. Nobody reads the third page.

## The Impact Formula

This is the single highest-leverage change most engineers can make to their resume.

The Google XYZ formula: "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]."

**Loses:** "Improved API performance"

**Wins:** "Reduced p95 API latency from 2.3s to 340ms by implementing connection pooling and query optimization, improving checkout conversion by 4%"

The second version has three things the first one doesn't: a starting number, an ending number, and a mechanism. It tells the reader what you actually did and why it mattered to the business.

You don't need metrics for every bullet. Some work genuinely doesn't have easy numbers. But anywhere you can attach a number — latency, throughput, error rate, cost, users, conversion rate, lines of code removed — do it. Specificity signals rigor. Vagueness signals that you either didn't measure or didn't understand what you built.

## Where to Put Your Tech Stack

Not at the bottom. Put a skills section near the top of your resume — after your name and contact info, before your work experience, or immediately after a one-line summary if you use one.

List languages, frameworks, and tools. Keep it accurate and current. Interviewers will ask about anything on that list, so don't pad it with things you used once in 2019. If you're not comfortable answering detailed questions about it, don't list it.

## What to Cut

- **Objective statements.** "Seeking a challenging role where I can leverage my skills..." Nobody reads these. Delete.
- **References section.** "Available upon request" is universally understood. The references section wastes space.
- **High school education.** Once you have a college degree, your high school is irrelevant. Remove it.
- **Outdated tech.** Listing "jQuery" as a core skill in 2026 hurts more than it helps unless you're explicitly in legacy maintenance work. It shifts attention toward old experience rather than current capability.
- **GPA.** Skip it unless it's above 3.7 and you're a recent grad. Below that threshold, it doesn't help. Above that threshold, it's a minor positive signal for new grads specifically.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Interviews

**Vague bullets.** "Worked on backend services" is not a bullet point. What did you build? What did it do? What changed because of it?

**Technologies without context.** Listing "Kubernetes, Terraform, AWS" in your experience bullets without explaining what you actually built with them tells the reader nothing. Infra buzzwords alone don't impress anyone past the initial screen.

**No quantified impact.** You worked there for two years. What's different now because of you? If you can't answer that question, work backwards — talk to your old team, dig up metrics, think about what you shipped and who used it.

**One resume for every job.** Sending a single generic resume to 500 companies is a worse strategy than sending a tailored resume to 50 companies. Read the job description. Mirror the language it uses when that language accurately describes your experience. Adjust your skills section. Move relevant experience higher. This takes 15 minutes per application and meaningfully improves response rates.

## The ATS Reality

Large companies use Applicant Tracking Systems to filter applications before a human ever sees them. ATS systems parse your resume and match keywords against the job description.

This means the language in the job posting matters. If a role says "experience with distributed systems" and your resume says "built scalable backend infrastructure," an ATS may not connect those. When the job description uses a specific term that accurately describes your experience, use that term.

Don't keyword stuff. Some ATS systems flag resumes that look artificially padded with keywords, and even the ones that don't — hiring managers notice when a resume reads like a keyword list rather than a career. Use the language naturally where it fits. Don't force it where it doesn't.

## Calibrating by Career Stage

**New grad:** Lead with projects and education. Internship experience matters a lot at this stage — put it prominently. Link your GitHub if the repos are clean and show real work. Coursework can appear if it's directly relevant, but only briefly.

**Mid-career (3–8 years):** Lead with work experience and impact bullets. Education moves to the bottom. Your internships are no longer relevant. Every bullet should be about what you shipped and what happened because of it.

**Senior and above:** Scope and leadership examples matter more. Who did you mentor? What systems did you own end-to-end? What architectural decisions did you drive? Individual contributor output still matters, but the reader is also evaluating whether you can operate at a higher level of abstraction.

## Cover Letters

In 2026, cover letters are optional at most companies and ignored at many. Recruiters often process applications in bulk through ATS — the cover letter field may not even be read before the first screen.

Write one when you have a specific reason to target that particular company that isn't obvious from your resume. A compelling cover letter can create a positive signal. A generic one ("I am excited to apply for the Software Engineer role at Acme Corp") is worse than nothing — it signals you didn't try.

## The Honest Rule

Your resume gets you the interview. Your interview gets you the offer.

Optimize the resume to get screened in, then trust your interview prep to close. A great resume that leads to a weak interview doesn't get you hired. An average resume that leads to an exceptional interview sometimes does.

Spend enough time on your resume to make it accurate, clear, and impact-focused — then spend the rest of your energy on interview practice.

That's where offers are actually won.
