# Questions to Ask the Hiring Manager: A Complete Guide

The questions you ask in an interview are as revealing as the answers you give. Strong candidates ask questions that demonstrate strategic thinking, genuine curiosity, and self-awareness. The wrong questions — or none at all — signal passivity. This guide covers what to ask the hiring manager specifically, as opposed to other interviewers.

## Why the Hiring Manager Round Is Different

The hiring manager is the person you will report to. Their answers reveal the actual experience of working on this team — the culture, the pace, the expectations, the growth path. Other interviewers tell you about the company; the hiring manager tells you about the job.

Treat this as a mutual evaluation. You are deciding whether you want this manager. Their answers should give you enough information to make that decision.

## Questions About the Role and Expectations

**"What does success look like in the first 90 days?"**
This is the most revealing question you can ask. The answer tells you: how structured onboarding is, what the team's immediate priorities are, and whether the manager has thought clearly about what they need from you. Vague answers are a yellow flag.

**"What are the biggest technical challenges the team is facing right now?"**
This gets you past the job description to the real work. The answer also tests whether the manager can speak technically, which tells you something about how close they are to the engineering.

**"How does engineering collaborate with product and design on this team?"**
The relationship between eng, product, and design is one of the most important factors in whether engineers feel ownership and agency. Healthy answers include engineers in product decisions; unhealthy answers describe engineers as order-takers.

**"How much time does the team spend on new features vs maintenance vs on-call?"**
This is a direct proxy for technical debt and work quality. A healthy ratio depends on the company, but if the answer is "most of our time is maintenance and incidents," that is significant information.

## Questions About the Manager

**"How do you typically give feedback to engineers on your team?"**
This tells you: how invested they are in your development, whether feedback is real-time or saved for annual reviews, and whether they are direct or conflict-averse.

**"What does growth look like for engineers at your level on this team?"**
How specific can they be? "We promote people to senior when they are ready" is vague. "Here is what we expect at senior and here is how we support the path" is a good manager.

**"What do you wish you knew about this role before you took it?"**
This is a surprisingly revealing question. Managers who answer honestly demonstrate self-awareness and trust. It often surfaces real information about the job's actual challenges.

**"What is one thing the team does really well, and one thing you are actively trying to improve?"**
Good managers can answer this immediately. The "improve" answer tells you what problems you will be working inside.

## Questions About the Team

**"What is the team's engineering culture like around code reviews, testing, and documentation?"**
These practices reveal whether the team takes quality seriously or ships and hopes. There is no universally correct answer — what matters is whether the answer matches your working style.

**"How long have the senior engineers on the team been here?"**
Tenure is a proxy for retention, which is a proxy for whether the team is a good place to work. High turnover in senior roles is a red flag, even if the company explains it away.

**"What is the on-call load like?"**
If the role involves on-call (most backend/infrastructure roles do), the frequency and severity of pages is critical information. Ask specifically: "How many pages per week on average? What is the typical incident severity?"

## Questions to Avoid

**"What does this company do?"** — You should know this. Not knowing is disqualifying.

**"What is the salary range?"** — This should happen with the recruiter before or after this round, not with the hiring manager.

**"Is there room for growth here?"** — This is too vague. Ask specifically about the promotion criteria or what the path from your level to the next looks like.

**Leading questions**: "You mentioned this is a great team — what makes it so great?" signals you have already decided rather than genuinely evaluating.

## Reading the Answers

The content of the answer matters, but so does the delivery:

- **Specific answers** signal a manager who thinks clearly about their team and their expectations
- **Immediate, unprompted answers** signal a manager who has considered these questions before
- **Hedged or vague answers** may signal confusion about the role, disorganization, or discomfort with transparency
- **Defensive answers** (when asked about challenges) signal a manager who cannot acknowledge problems — rarely a good sign

You are not looking for perfect answers. You are building a data picture of whether this is a team you want to join and a manager you want to work for. The questions in this guide generate that data.
