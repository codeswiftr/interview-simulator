# Advanced Job Offer Negotiation: Equity Analysis, Competing Offers, and Total Compensation Strategy

Most negotiation advice covers the basics: always counter, know your market rate, don't share your current salary. This guide assumes you know those things and covers what actually matters at senior levels — equity analysis, multi-offer leverage, and the strategic moves that affect outcomes by $50,000-$200,000 over a four-year vesting period.

## Understanding Total Compensation Components

Before negotiating, understand what you're negotiating.

**Base salary**: Cash. Predictable. Used to calculate benefits (401k matching %, severance). Raises compound from the base, so a higher base compounds over time. Not the highest-leverage number for senior engineers at high-growth companies, but still meaningful.

**Equity**: The high-variance, high-leverage component. Three forms:

**RSUs (Restricted Stock Units)**: Grants of actual shares that vest over time (typically 4-year vesting, 1-year cliff). Value = (number of shares) × (stock price at vesting). At public companies, you can calculate the current value with certainty. At pre-IPO companies, the value depends entirely on the exit outcome.

**Stock options (ISOs/NSOs)**: The right to buy shares at a fixed price (strike price, usually the 409A valuation at grant date). Value at exercise = (fair market value - strike price) × (number of shares). Options can expire (typically 10 years, but post-termination exercise windows may be only 90 days — a critical detail that traps many employees).

**Target bonus**: At large companies, a percentage of base salary (10-25% for senior engineers). Usually tied to company and individual performance. Less predictable than base but still meaningful.

**The equity comparison problem**: Comparing offers across companies is genuinely difficult. A private company's "option strike price $1.00, current 409A $10.00" sounds great, but if it never exits, it's worthless. A public company's RSU at $200/share in two years could be worth $400 or $100.

## Equity Analysis for Pre-IPO Companies

For pre-IPO equity, you need to understand:

1. **Strike price vs. FMV (409A)**: The spread is your intrinsic value. $1 strike on stock with a $10 409A valuation = $9 of intrinsic value per option.

2. **Preference stack**: How much in liquidation preferences sit above common stock (your options). A company with $100M raised at 2x liquidation preference needs to exit at >$200M before common stock sees any value. Ask for the cap table and preference structure.

3. **Current 409A valuation**: The 409A is the IRS-approved fair market value, updated periodically. Offers are often described in shares, not dollars — ask what the current 409A valuation is to calculate the current equity value.

4. **Post-termination exercise window (PTEW)**: If you leave the company before IPO, most options expire 90 days after your last day. A company that extends this to 5 or 10 years is a meaningful differentiator — early-stage employees who leave 2-3 years in often lose all their equity because they can't afford to exercise options and pay taxes before a liquidity event.

5. **Dilution**: Your percentage ownership will dilute with future funding rounds. Ask "what's the current fully diluted share count?" to calculate your ownership percentage.

## Leveraging Competing Offers

The single most effective negotiation lever is having a competing offer from a company you'd genuinely join. Everything else is weaker.

**Timeline management**: If you're interested in multiple companies, start processes in parallel. Let recruiters know you have competing timelines: "I have another process completing around [date]." This creates urgency without being dishonest.

**How to share the competing offer**: "I have an offer from [Company X] for $[amount total comp]. This company is my first choice for [specific reasons], but I need to make the numbers work." You're not threatening to leave — you're asking for help closing the gap.

**Be honest about what you'd accept**: Recruiters are better at getting approvals when they know exactly what they need. "If you can get to $X total comp, I'll sign tomorrow" is more effective than vague back-and-forth.

**Competing offers matter more than market data**: "Levels.fyi shows this role pays $400K" is weaker than "I have an offer for $400K from [Company]." Competing offers are real and verifiable. Market data is abstract.

## What to Negotiate Beyond Base

Especially at companies with fixed salary bands, there's often more flexibility in other components:

**Equity refresh**: Starting equity is negotiable, but ongoing refresh grants are also worth negotiating upfront. Ask: "What is the typical annual refresh grant for this level?"

**Sign-on bonus**: Used to offset unvested equity you're leaving behind. If you're forfeiting RSUs or unvested options, calculate the value at current price and ask for a sign-on to offset it. Companies expect this.

**Vesting schedule**: 4-year with 1-year cliff is standard. Some companies will negotiate a shorter cliff (6 months) for senior candidates, or accelerated vesting on acquisition.

**Start date and vacation**: If you need specific start date flexibility or have a vacation planned in year one, negotiate this before signing — it's much harder to negotiate afterward.

**Remote flexibility**: If not in the offer letter, negotiate explicit work-from-home expectations in writing before you sign.

## Handling Exploding Offers and Deadlines

"This offer expires Friday" is a negotiation tactic, not a law. You can almost always get an extension.

**How to ask**: "I'm very excited about this offer and want to make sure I give it the consideration it deserves. Can I have [additional time]?" Most companies will extend 1-2 weeks without question.

**If the deadline is genuine**: Recruiting teams have reasons for real deadlines (headcount freezing, budget cycles). If they say they genuinely can't extend, you need to decide with the information you have. Don't let manufactured urgency cause you to accept a worse offer — but also don't assume all deadlines are fake.

**The counter-offer from your current employer**: If you were not planning to leave, now you're getting a retention offer. Evaluate honestly: why were you interviewing? If the answer is career growth or culture, a salary increase doesn't address those issues. Many engineers who accept counter-offers leave anyway within 6 months.

## The Number That Matters

Total compensation over the vesting period (4 years at most companies) is the number to compare. Add up: (base × 4) + (equity value) + (signing bonus) + (expected annual bonus × 4). Compare offers on this basis, not on any single component.

Then apply your discount rate to private equity — the further from liquidity, the larger the discount. A pre-Series B startup's equity might deserve a 70% discount versus a public company's RSUs. A pre-IPO unicorn with a confirmed IPO timeline might deserve a 30% discount.

Negotiation at senior levels is a skill worth developing deliberately. Engineers who negotiate well over their careers consistently earn $500K-$2M more over a decade than those who accept first offers. The discomfort of negotiating lasts 30 minutes. The financial impact lasts years.
