# Interview Prep for International Engineers: H-1B, Visas, and What Changes

International engineers make up a significant portion of the U.S. tech workforce, and many of the most competitive candidates in the market are on visas or preparing to relocate. But the logistics of visa sponsorship, work authorization, and the specific dynamics of interviewing as an international candidate add a layer of complexity that most interview guides ignore entirely. This guide covers what actually changes — and what doesn't — when you're interviewing as an international engineer.

## What Doesn't Change: The Technical Bar

The most important thing to understand first: the technical bar is identical for international and domestic candidates. Algorithmic coding questions, system design, behavioral interviews — all of these are evaluated the same way. No interviewer is scoring you on a different rubric because of your visa status.

The skills that matter remain the same: data structures and algorithms, system design reasoning, communication clarity, and evidence of genuine engineering capability. If anything, international engineers who have gone through selective university programs in India, China, Europe, or elsewhere often arrive with stronger algorithmic fundamentals than the average domestic candidate. The technical preparation strategy is identical.

## The Sponsorship Question: When and How to Mention It

The question of when to disclose that you need visa sponsorship is one of the most common anxieties for international candidates. The answer depends on your specific situation:

**If you're on OPT/STEM OPT**: You are authorized to work right now without any company sponsorship action. You become an H-1B case in the future. Some candidates on STEM OPT delay mentioning future H-1B needs until after receiving an offer. This is a judgment call — being upfront avoids wasted time if a company categorically doesn't sponsor, but mentioning it too early can filter you before your technical skills are evaluated.

**If you need H-1B sponsorship from day one**: Be upfront before the technical interview process begins, not after an offer. Some companies categorically don't sponsor (smaller companies, some startups). Finding this out after investing 5 rounds of interviews is painful for everyone. Most job application forms ask directly — answer honestly.

**If you have a green card or EAD**: You're authorized to work without restriction. You don't need to mention immigration status at all.

The practical advice: don't lead with the sponsorship question in your initial outreach, but address it early if it comes up or if you're applying to companies known to be sponsorship-averse.

## Companies That Sponsor vs. Companies That Don't

Large tech companies (FAANG+, major tech platforms, financial technology companies) routinely sponsor H-1B visas because the talent pool they're drawing from requires it. Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple, Stripe, Uber, Airbnb, Salesforce — these companies have established immigration programs and legal teams.

Companies that typically don't sponsor: small startups (under ~50 employees), most non-tech companies trying to hire a single engineer, companies in regulated industries that have internal complexity around international hires.

The trickiest category: mid-size companies (50-500 employees). Their policy varies. The H-1B process costs a company $5,000-$10,000+ in legal fees per application plus the lottery uncertainty. Some companies see this as a reasonable cost for good talent; others avoid it.

## The H-1B Lottery Problem

The H-1B lottery — where 85,000 cap-subject visas are allocated by lottery from hundreds of thousands of applications — creates genuine uncertainty. In recent years, the lottery odds have been roughly 1-in-3 for a single application, and 1-in-6 for OPT holders who haven't done a master's degree.

For job searches, this means: if you're on OPT and your company needs to enter you in the lottery, you may not know until April whether you'll be able to continue working past October. This uncertainty is real and you can't eliminate it, but you can be transparent with employers about the timeline, maximize your chances by applying early, and target companies with experience managing H-1B transitions.

Cap-exempt employers (universities, research institutions, nonprofit research organizations) are not subject to the lottery — if working in academia is an option, it removes the lottery risk entirely.

## Preparing for U.S. Interviews From Abroad

Engineers preparing for U.S. tech company interviews from outside the U.S. face some specific challenges:

**LeetCode-style interviews are U.S.-centric**: Many global tech companies (especially European ones) use more practical interview formats. If you're targeting U.S. companies, the investment in algorithmic interview preparation is necessary even if it seems disconnected from real work.

**Communication style**: U.S. technical interviews reward thinking out loud — explaining your reasoning as you work through a problem, asking clarifying questions before coding, discussing trade-offs before committing to an approach. This collaborative style differs from interview formats in some other countries where candidates are expected to arrive at an answer silently and present it.

**Time zone management**: Scheduling interviews across 10-12 hour time differences is physically challenging. Build buffer into your schedule, communicate timezone clearly, and try to get morning slots in the U.S. (which may be evening for you) rather than 6 AM your time.

**Remote-first companies**: Several large tech companies have committed to fully remote or distributed work. These companies are significantly more open to hiring international engineers without requiring relocation, and the U.S. presence required for H-1B doesn't apply if the company sponsors a different visa category or you're working from your home country as a contractor.

## The Long Game: Building Toward Permanent Residency

For engineers who want to stay in the U.S. long-term, the H-1B is a waypoint, not the destination. The green card (permanent residency) process — typically via EB-2 or EB-3 employment-based categories — involves a PERM labor certification, company sponsorship, and for engineers from high-demand countries (India, China), backlogs that currently run decades.

This is worth knowing early: the company you join and how quickly they initiate the green card process matters significantly for your long-term trajectory. Large tech companies with established immigration teams often start the process quickly. Smaller companies may not, which can cost years.

The technical interview preparation strategy is unchanged by all of this — you need to be technically excellent to get offers in the first place. But understanding the visa and immigration context helps you direct your job search, ask the right questions in offer negotiations, and make choices that align with your long-term goals.
