The first 90 days at a new engineering job set the trajectory for everything that follows. Engineers who ramp up effectively early are trusted with more responsibility sooner. Those who struggle — even if technically strong — often find themselves working uphill for months. The difference is rarely technical skill; it's intentionality and strategy.

## Days 1–30: Learn Before You Lead

Your first month has one primary goal: understand before you act. Resist the urge to immediately propose changes or "improvements" to the codebase or process. You don't yet have the context to know whether the things that look wrong are actually wrong, or whether they're the result of hard-won decisions made months ago.

**Understand the technical landscape:**
- Read the system design docs, ADRs, and any architecture documents you can find
- Pull the codebase and get it running locally on day one — if you can't, that's your first contribution
- Trace a request end-to-end through the system before you touch anything
- Find out how code gets from a developer's machine to production: CI/CD pipeline, deployment process, feature flags, rollback procedure

**Understand the business context:**
- What does the team's product or service actually do for users or customers?
- What metrics does the team track and care about?
- What's in the current sprint, and why were those things prioritized?

**Find your onboarding document and your 30/60/90-day expectations.** If they don't exist, ask your manager explicitly: "What does success look like at 30 days?" Good managers have an answer. If they don't, that's information too — you'll need to define your own milestones.

## Relationships Are Infrastructure

Technical contributions get noticed, but relationships determine whether you thrive long-term. Invest in them deliberately.

**Schedule 1-on-1s with everyone on your immediate team** in the first two weeks. Not to show them how good you are — to listen. Ask: What are you working on? What's the hardest part of this team's work right now? What do you wish you'd known when you joined?

**Find your key stakeholders:** Who does your team frequently interface with — product, design, data, infra? Get a brief introduction meeting with one or two people in each group. You're building a map of who to talk to when you need something.

**Find a peer mentor.** Most teams have a mid-level engineer who is deeply knowledgeable and willing to help. They're often more approachable than senior engineers and can answer questions that feel too "basic" to bring to your manager.

**Understand the informal culture:** How do people actually communicate? Is Slack the real decision-making channel, or does that happen in meetings? How are disagreements resolved? Who has influence that isn't reflected in their title? This context is only learned by observation, not documentation.

## Days 31–60: Deliver Something Meaningful

By day 31, you should have enough context to be productive. This is when you shift from primarily learning to primarily contributing — while still learning.

**Own something end-to-end.** A feature, a bug fix, an infrastructure improvement. The size matters less than the ownership. See it from ticket to production, including code review, testing, deployment, and verification. This is how you build credibility and understand the real workflow.

**Fix things that bothered you in the first month.** Unclear documentation, an intermittently failing test, a confusing error message, a script that every new engineer asks about. These "broken window" contributions are high-value and demonstrate that you've been paying attention. Document your fixes — they're also useful for any future new hire.

**Ask questions publicly.** Post questions in team channels rather than always DMing one person. When you figure out the answer, post that too. You'll establish yourself as someone who both asks good questions and closes the loop.

## Days 61–90: Build Leverage

By month three, you should understand the codebase well enough to have opinions about what should change. This is when to shift from reactive work (pick up tickets) to proactive contributions.

**Identify one medium-term improvement and propose it.** A refactor that would remove a category of bugs, a missing piece of observability, a test coverage gap in a critical path. Write up a brief proposal — even just a Slack message or a short doc. This signals that you're thinking about the system's health, not just completing tasks.

**Become the expert in something.** You can't be the expert in everything yet, but you can own a slice. A module, a service, a type of problem. Deep ownership in one area builds more credibility faster than shallow familiarity everywhere.

**Revisit your 30/60/90-day goals with your manager.** What did you accomplish? What did you not get to? What are the goals for the next quarter? This conversation establishes you as someone who manages their own performance proactively — which is what senior engineers do.

## Managing Your Manager

Your relationship with your manager is the highest-leverage relationship in your first 90 days. Invest in making it productive.

**Communicate proactively.** Don't wait for your weekly 1-on-1 to surface blockers. A quick Slack message — "I'm blocked on X, trying Y" — keeps your manager informed without requiring a meeting. It also demonstrates that you're moving forward, not stuck.

**Understand their priorities.** Your manager is being judged by their own manager on specific outcomes. Understanding those outcomes helps you prioritize your work to create the most visibility and value.

**Ask for feedback explicitly.** "Is there anything I should be doing differently?" is a question most managers won't answer unless asked. Make it a standing question in your monthly 1-on-1s. The feedback you get early is much easier to act on than feedback you get at a year-end review.

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **The hero move:** Proposing a massive rewrite of a core system in week two. You don't have the context to know why it is the way it is.
- **The invisible ramp:** Working quietly, producing nothing visible for the first 6 weeks, then wondering why you don't feel trusted.
- **Skipping the small stuff:** Treating documentation fixes and test improvements as below you. They're not. They demonstrate attention to quality.
- **Over-optimizing for looking good:** Prioritizing visible tasks over necessary work. Your teammates notice the gap between performance and substance.

The engineers who thrive in their first 90 days are curious, deliberate, and generous with credit. They contribute to the codebase and to the team's knowledge. They build trust by following through on small commitments before asking for big ones. That foundation pays compounding returns for the rest of your tenure.
