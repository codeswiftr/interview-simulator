# What Happens After You Get the Offer: A Complete Guide

Getting an offer is the moment most interview guides treat as the finish line. It isn't. The decisions you make in the days and weeks between receiving an offer and starting a new job have significant financial and career consequences. This guide covers what actually happens after the offer call — and how to handle each step well.

## The Offer Call: What to Do and Not Do

When a recruiter or hiring manager calls with an offer, resist the urge to respond immediately. The professional expectation is that you say something like: "Thank you — I'm genuinely excited. Can I have a few days to review the details?" Every legitimate employer will say yes.

What to never do on the offer call: negotiate in real time without the written offer in front of you. You'll make mistakes, accept things you can't evaluate, and lose leverage you didn't know you had. Get the written offer first.

What to clarify on the call: the deadline for your response (ask if you're not told — most companies give 3-5 business days), the full compensation breakdown you'll receive in writing, and whether there's flexibility on start date.

## Understanding Total Compensation

Base salary is one component of a compensation package that typically includes several others, and the differences between offers are often in the components candidates evaluate least carefully.

**Equity**: For public company employees, the value of RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) is calculable: shares × current stock price. The vesting schedule matters — a 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff means you receive nothing if you leave before 12 months, then 25% at the cliff, then monthly or quarterly thereafter. For private company stock options, valuation requires understanding the strike price, the company's latest 409A valuation, and the liquidation preference stack.

**Signing bonus**: Often one-time. Understand the clawback — most companies require repayment if you leave within 1-2 years. A $30,000 signing bonus with a 1-year clawback is essentially a $30K bridge loan if you leave early.

**Annual bonus**: Variable. What is the target, what is the range, and what has been the historical payout? "Target 15%" at a company that has hit 100% of target bonus 3 years in a row is different from the same number at a company that hasn't paid above 70%.

**Benefits**: Health insurance quality varies significantly. A plan with $200/month employee contribution and low deductibles is worth thousands of dollars annually compared to a $400/month plan with a $3,000 deductible. Add up the real cost before comparing.

## Negotiation: What Actually Works

Negotiation after an offer is normal, expected, and rarely results in offers being rescinded (this essentially never happens for reasonable counter-requests). The question is how to negotiate effectively.

**The single counter**: Present one clear ask rather than a list. "I'd like to increase the base to $X" or "I'd like an additional $Y in signing bonus" is more effective than itemizing multiple requests. Recruiters can champion one ask to hiring managers; multiple requests create friction.

**Anchoring**: Counter above your target. If your target is $200K and the offer is $185K, counter at $210K rather than $200K — leaves room for the company to "meet you in the middle" at $197-203K.

**The competing offer**: The most powerful leverage is a competing offer at a higher number. "I have an offer from [Company] at $X — can you come closer to that?" gives recruiters something concrete to take to hiring managers. You don't have to accept the competing offer; you have to have it.

**What not to say**: "I have bills" or "I really need more money" is not effective leverage. Business reasons (competing offer, current compensation, market rate data from levels.fyi) work; personal financial need does not.

## Handling Multiple Offers

If you're fortunate enough to have competing offers, the decision requires comparing more than the numbers:

**The team you're joining**: Spend time during the interview process evaluating the team. Who will you be working with directly? How do they communicate, make decisions, handle disagreement? The team has more impact on your daily experience than the company.

**Growth trajectory**: Where does the role lead? What roles have people who were in this position 2-3 years ago moved into? A lower-compensation offer with a clear path to a senior role you want may be better than a higher-compensation lateral move.

**The 18-month test**: "Which of these offers will I be most proud of having taken in 18 months?" This cuts through compensation fixation and focuses on career development.

Managing competing offer deadlines: be transparent with recruiters. "I have another offer with a deadline of Thursday — can you accelerate your process?" Most recruiters will try to accommodate. Don't fabricate deadlines — recruiters talk to each other and misrepresentation damages your reputation.

## The Notice Period and Transition

Standard notice is two weeks in the U.S. (longer in Europe). Your obligations during notice:

- Complete ongoing work to a handoff-ready state
- Document what you know that others don't
- Don't take confidential information or code when you leave
- Don't recruit colleagues during the notice period

Counteroffer from your current employer: be cautious. Research consistently shows that most engineers who accept counteroffers leave within 18 months anyway — the underlying reasons for leaving rarely change. Evaluate whether the counteroffer addresses the actual reason you were looking.

## Setting Up Your First 90 Days

The offer and start date gap is when most engineers think about vacation. The engineers who start strong also do some preparation:

- Read the company's engineering blog to understand what problems they're working on
- Set up your development environment before day one if possible (most companies send equipment early)
- Plan your first week to listen more than you contribute — your initial impressions of what needs changing are usually wrong until you understand the context

The 90-day rule: resist the urge to propose major changes in the first month. The best first 90 days involve shipping something small and useful, building relationships, and understanding why the system is the way it is before deciding how it should change.

The offer is the beginning of the next chapter. How you negotiate it, evaluate it, and prepare for it determines how the chapter starts.
