# Navigating Tech Layoffs: A Recovery Guide

Tech layoffs have become a regular feature of the industry. 2022-2024 saw over 500,000 tech workers laid off globally. If you've been laid off, or you're worried about it, this guide is for you. Being laid off from a tech job is not the end of your career—for many engineers, it's the start of the next chapter.

## The First 24-48 Hours

Before anything else, take care of the logistics:

**1. Document everything before you lose access**: Take screenshots of your work, save contact information of colleagues, export any portfolio-worthy projects (with permission—don't take code). You'll lose access to email, code repos, and systems within hours.

**2. Understand your severance package**: Read the agreement carefully before signing. Key elements: severance amount, COBRA health insurance eligibility, vesting acceleration (check if any options accelerate), non-compete clauses (rare but exist), and equity treatment (what happens to unvested options).

**3. Don't sign immediately if you feel pressured**: Most severance agreements give you 21+ days to sign. Companies cannot legally compel you to sign on the day of notification.

**4. File for unemployment**: Do this within the first week. In the US, tech workers qualify for state unemployment benefits. Severance may delay when benefits start in some states, but file anyway.

## Financial Triage

Knowing your runway is essential—it determines your job search strategy.

**Calculate your runway**:
- Monthly expenses (fixed + variable)
- Liquid assets (cash, accessible investments—not retirement accounts)
- Severance amount
- Expected monthly unemployment benefit

At 6 months' runway: you have breathing room for a selective, high-quality job search.
At 2-3 months: you need to move fast; consider contracting work to extend runway while searching.
At < 1 month: prioritize income over perfect-fit roles; a good job > no job.

**Reduce burn rate immediately**: Audit subscriptions, pause non-essential recurring expenses. This isn't about panic—it's about buying time for a better search.

## The Job Search: What Actually Works in 2026

**The hard truth**: The spray-and-pray approach (apply to 200 jobs on LinkedIn) has poor conversion rates, especially for senior roles. The highest-converting path is warm referrals from your professional network.

**Activate your network first**:
- Message former colleagues and managers: "I was just laid off from [Company]. I'm looking for senior backend roles. Know anyone I should talk to?"
- Be specific about what you want—"senior backend engineer at a Series B-D startup" converts better than "I'm open to anything"
- Post on LinkedIn: a genuine, non-dramatic post about being open to opportunities gets significant engagement

**The referral advantage**: At most tech companies, referred candidates get their resumes read and move to phone screen at 3-5x the rate of cold applications. One referral is worth 50 cold applications.

**Cold outreach that works**:
- Target hiring managers (not HR) at companies you genuinely want to join
- Personalized note referencing their tech stack, recent blog post, or product
- 2-3 sentences max; end with a clear, low-friction ask ("Would you be open to a 20-minute call?")

## Framing the Layoff in Interviews

Layoffs in tech are common enough that the stigma has largely evaporated. How to address it:

**Simple, matter-of-fact framing**: "There was a company-wide reduction in force as [Company] rightsized after the growth period. My entire [team/department] was impacted." This is accurate, non-defensive, and redirects to the work quickly.

**What not to do**: Don't over-explain, don't criticize the company, don't express bitterness. Move to what you're looking for next within two sentences.

**When asked what you've been doing since**:
- Honest: job searching, maintaining skills, recovering from a stressful event
- Additive: if you've done open source contributions, freelance work, or studying, mention those
- Interviewers respect honesty far more than a polished cover story

## Using the Time Well

If you have runway (3+ months), treat some of this time as an investment:

**Technical depth**: Study a system design area you've never had time for. Build a project that demonstrates a skill you want more interviews around.

**Interview preparation**: A layoff gives you the time to prepare properly. Do structured LeetCode sessions daily. Practice system design with a partner. Record yourself doing behavioral interviews.

**Network investment**: Attend local meetups, write a blog post about something you built, contribute to open source. These activities generate passive inbound over the next 6-12 months.

## The Emotional Dimension

This is real and should be acknowledged: being laid off is a significant stressor. Even when it's entirely impersonal (and most tech layoffs are), it disrupts identity, routine, and financial security.

**What helps**:
- Maintaining structure (wake time, exercise, dedicated work hours on the search)
- Connecting with others who've been through it (communities, Slack groups)
- Setting realistic expectations (a strong job search takes 2-4 months, not 2 weeks)
- Reframing: you're not damaged goods; you're a skilled engineer in a market that values your skills

**What hurts**:
- Checking LinkedIn obsessively throughout the day
- Comparing your timeline to others
- Taking every rejection personally

## Turning It Into a Career Accelerator

Many engineers look back on being laid off as the best thing that happened to their career:
- Forced them out of a role they'd stayed in too long
- Created time to interview broadly and find a role that truly fit
- Led to compensation increases (job changes are historically the biggest salary lever)
- Opened doors to company stages, domains, or roles they'd never have sought actively

The engineers who use layoffs as accelerators are the ones who approach the search actively and don't just wait for the perfect role to appear.

Take the time. Do the prep. Be intentional about what you want next. The job market for skilled engineers is resilient, even when tech hiring contracts. Your skills don't expire—and your next role might be significantly better than the one you just left.
