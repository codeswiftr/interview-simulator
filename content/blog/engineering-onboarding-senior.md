# Senior Engineer Onboarding: How to Ramp Up at a New Company in 90 Days

Senior engineers often underestimate how much an onboarding mindset matters. The skills that made you successful at your last company don't automatically translate — you need to understand the specific codebase, team dynamics, deployment processes, and unwritten norms before you can contribute at your level. This guide gives you a 90-day framework to ramp up effectively.

## Days 1-30: Listen, Learn, Map

The first month is for understanding, not building. Resist the impulse to start proposing solutions before you fully understand the problems.

**Build your technical map.** Draw the system architecture as you understand it. Every service, every data store, every queue, every external dependency. Label what you don't understand yet. Update it daily. By week 4, you should have a complete picture. This map becomes your most valuable artifact — it reveals blind spots in your understanding and gives you conversation topics with domain experts.

**Read everything you can find.** Architecture decision records (ADRs), post-mortems, the wiki, old Slack threads. The history of technical decisions tells you more about the system than the code does. You'll understand not just how things work but why they were built that way. Post-mortems are especially valuable — they reveal the failure modes the team has learned to fear.

**Do the obvious beginner tasks.** Set up your development environment. Get a small bug fix or feature through the entire deployment pipeline. This reveals the actual workflow (CI/CD, code review norms, deployment gates) that you'll operate in daily. Don't skip this because it feels below your level — you need to understand the process before you can improve it.

**Map the humans, not just the systems.** Who are the domain experts for each component? Who makes architectural decisions? Who is the technical authority for each major system? Who can answer questions quickly? Build this knowledge deliberately — it's as important as the technical map.

## Days 31-60: Pick Up Weight Gradually

By week 5-6, you should be contributing meaningfully. The goal is to start demonstrating competence while still learning.

**Take on a medium-complexity task.** Not a trivial fix, but not the most critical system either. Something that lets you write production code, get reviewed, iterate, and ship. This establishes your code quality standards and review style within the team.

**Start asking better questions.** In week 1, questions were "how does X work?" By week 5, they should be "I noticed X and Y, which makes me think we're doing Z — am I reading this right?" This shift signals that you're synthesizing, not just absorbing.

**Identify the debt.** Every codebase has areas that everyone knows are problematic but nobody has time to fix. Find them. Don't immediately start fixing them, but note them. Understanding the historical debt — and why it exists — tells you a lot about the team's priorities and constraints.

**Find your first high-impact contribution.** Look for problems where your external perspective is an asset. What do you see that insiders have normalized? Fresh eyes catch things that teams have stopped seeing. This is where senior hires often deliver their first real value.

## Days 61-90: Drive, Don't Just Follow

By day 60, you should be fully ramped on the critical systems and have shipped several meaningful changes. Now shift to a more proactive stance.

**Propose your first architectural initiative.** Based on your observations, propose an improvement — performance improvement, reliability fix, process change, refactor. Write it up properly: current state, problem, proposed solution, alternatives considered, risks. The quality of this document signals your senior engineering caliber more than any code change.

**Take on a leadership role in an ongoing project.** Volunteer to lead a project, coordinate a cross-team initiative, or drive a technical decision process. This demonstrates that you're operating at senior level, not just delivering as a strong individual contributor.

**Establish your office hours.** Make yourself available as a resource for your immediate team. Answer questions, unblock others, review code thoroughly. This builds your reputation and gives you visibility into the problems the team faces daily.

## Common Senior Engineer Onboarding Mistakes

**Moving too fast.** Proposing major changes in week 2 before you understand why things are the way they are. This is the most common mistake. Engineers who came from strong technical cultures often assume their previous patterns are universally correct.

**Not investing in relationships.** Technical credibility is necessary but insufficient. You need to be trusted, not just technically respected. Coffee conversations, 1:1s with teammates, and genuine curiosity about people's work all matter.

**Avoiding the slow systems.** Every codebase has systems that are "known problems" — ancient monoliths, unmaintained services, brittle pipelines. Avoiding them limits your understanding. Carefully engaging with them, even to just understand and document, builds significant credibility.

**Comparing to the previous company.** "At my last company, we did it this way" is the most counterproductive phrase in an engineer's first 90 days. Even if your previous approach was better, you need to understand the context before advocating for change.

**Optimizing for looking productive over being productive.** Shipping many small PRs in week 1 looks active but may not build the foundation you need. The highest-value use of your first month is understanding — even if that produces fewer visible artifacts.

## The 90-Day Output

By day 90, you should have:
- A complete technical map of the systems you'll work with
- Several shipped contributions, including at least one of medium complexity
- One meaningful technical proposal or initiative in progress
- Strong working relationships with your immediate team
- A clear view of the highest-impact problems you can tackle in months 3-6

The engineers who ramp fastest are those who treat onboarding as a discipline, not a phase to get through.
