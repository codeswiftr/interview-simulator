# Salary Negotiation for Senior Engineers: Maximize Your Offer

Senior engineer salaries have high variance. Two engineers with identical skills accepting the same role at the same company can end up with total compensation 20-40% apart, purely based on negotiation skill. This guide covers what actually works.

## The Fundamental Principle: You Don't Negotiate Once, You Create Leverage

Negotiation isn't a single conversation at the end. It's a process that starts the moment you begin interviewing. The single most powerful lever: **competing offers**.

A company that knows you have an offer from a competitor has a concrete decision to make: match or lose you. A company negotiating against an abstract "market value" can dismiss your request. Companies hate losing candidates they've invested 5+ interview rounds in — use that.

## How to Build Competing Offers

Don't start interviewing at your dream company first. Start with companies that are slightly less preferred. Complete those processes first — you may get offers 3-4 weeks before your target company.

When you get your first offer, tell all companies you're in process with: "I have an offer with a deadline of [date]. I want to give you the opportunity to move quickly if there's interest." This accelerates processes and creates a forcing function.

Maintain at least 2-3 active processes simultaneously. One offer is a data point. Two offers are leverage. Three offers mean companies are competing for you.

## The Comp Stack

Senior engineer total compensation has several components:

**Base salary:** Predictable, liquid, annualized. Easier to negotiate at most companies than equity.

**Signing bonus:** One-time payment at joining. Often used to offset unvested equity from previous employer. Typically repaid if you leave within 12-24 months. Highly negotiable — companies have budget here even when base salary bands are fixed.

**Annual bonus:** Target percentage of base. At FAANG it's typically 10-25% of base. Often non-negotiable as a percentage (it's a policy), but you can negotiate the base it's applied to.

**RSUs (Restricted Stock Units):** Equity vested over 4 years (typically 1-year cliff then quarterly). The headline number is the total grant value at current stock price. Know the vesting schedule, refresh grants policy, and what happens if you're asked to leave.

**Total Compensation (TC):** Base + expected bonus + annual RSU vest. This is how engineers at top companies compare offers. $200K TC could be $180K base + $20K bonus, or $150K base + $50K bonus + $0 RSUs — very different in practice.

## Negotiation Tactics

**Never be the first to name a number.** "What are you looking for?" — "I'm focused on the opportunity and would love to hear your best offer first." If forced to give a range, give a range where your floor is their ceiling.

**Always negotiate, even if you're happy with the offer.** "I'm excited about this role. The offer is close to what I was hoping for — is there flexibility on [base/equity/signing bonus]?" You lose nothing by asking. Recruiters expect it.

**Be specific.** "I'd need to see $210K base (up from $195K)" is more effective than "I was hoping for a bit more." Specific asks are easier to approve or counter-propose than vague requests.

**Use your competing offer explicitly.** "I have an offer from [Competitor] at $230K TC. I'd prefer to join your company — can you match or get close to $235K TC?" This is direct, professional, and effective.

**Negotiate the whole package.** If they won't move on base, ask for more signing bonus, or more equity, or faster vesting, or better refresh grants. Something in the package is usually flexible.

## Handling Exploding Offers

Exploding offers (deadlines of 48-72 hours) are a pressure tactic to prevent you from getting competing offers. They're negotiable.

"I'm very interested in this opportunity. My current timeline puts me finishing two other processes in 10 days — could we extend the deadline to [specific date]? I want to make a fully informed decision."

Most companies will extend. If they refuse, that's a signal about how they'll treat you as an employee. A company that pressures you before you're even hired will pressure you after.

## Equity Negotiation Specifics

**What to evaluate:** Company stage (public vs private changes liquidity), stock price trend, vesting schedule, cliff period, refresh grants, acceleration on acquisition.

**Negotiating equity amount:** "Given my experience level, I was expecting a grant closer to [X] RSUs. The current offer is [Y]. Is there room to get closer to [X]?"

**Cliff waiver:** If you're leaving unvested RSUs, ask for an accelerated vesting start or signing bonus to compensate. "I'll be leaving $80K in unvested RSUs. A signing bonus of $40-50K would help bridge the gap."

## Counter-Offer from Current Employer

When you give notice, your current employer may make a counter-offer. Data consistently shows that 80%+ of people who accept counter-offers leave within 12 months anyway.

The underlying issues that made you look for a new job don't change because of a salary bump. Additionally, your employer now knows you were looking — trust is damaged. Accepting a counter-offer usually just delays the inevitable.

Use it as leverage if you want to: inform competing offers about the counter-offer. But rarely actually accept it.

## The Conversation Script

Initial response to offer: "Thank you — I'm really excited about this opportunity. I'd like a few days to review the details carefully. Can you send the full offer letter?" (Never respond to an offer verbally in the same call.)

Counter-offer call: "Hi [Recruiter], I've had a chance to review the offer and I'm genuinely excited about the role and team. The offer is competitive but I was hoping we could work toward [specific ask]. Is there flexibility there?"

If they say no: "I understand. Is there flexibility in [other component]? I want to make this work." If they say yes: "That would work well — I'm ready to move forward."

After negotiation: "I accept. I'm looking forward to joining the team." Keep it gracious — you'll be working with these people.
