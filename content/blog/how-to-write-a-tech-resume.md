# How to Write a Software Engineering Resume That Gets Past Screening

Most engineering resumes fail before a human reads them. The reasons are predictable, and they have nothing to do with the candidate's ability to do the job. This guide covers what actually matters in a software engineering resume at different career stages and how to structure yours to pass both automated and human screening.

## What Screeners Are Looking For (And What They're Not)

Recruiters and ATS systems screen for two things: signals of technical relevance and signals of impact. Technical relevance means: does this person have experience with the technologies we use? Impact means: did this person's work matter?

What screeners are not looking for: clever formatting, creative layouts, objective statements, or personality summaries. These are noise. The information that matters fits on one page (two pages for 10+ years of experience) in a clean, readable format.

The most important real estate on an engineering resume is the first bullet under your most recent job. That bullet will be read. Everything else might be scanned.

## The Impact Bullet Formula

The standard "responsible for X" bullet is worthless. It tells a recruiter what your job description was, not what you accomplished. The format that works:

**[Action verb] [what you built/changed] [the result, with a number where possible]**

Examples:
- "Redesigned the checkout flow, reducing cart abandonment by 18% and increasing checkout completion from 61% to 72%"
- "Built a caching layer for the product catalog API, reducing p95 latency from 420ms to 38ms"
- "Led migration of 14 microservices from Python 2 to Python 3, eliminating 200+ deprecation warnings and enabling upgrade to Django 4.0"

If you cannot put a number, use a scale signal: "used by 3M monthly active users," "processing 50K transactions/day," "covering 40+ API endpoints." Even approximate scale is better than no scale.

If you genuinely have no metrics, use qualitative outcomes that are still specific: "adopted as the standard pattern across 3 engineering teams," "reduced on-call incidents from weekly to monthly."

## What to Include by Experience Level

**New grad (0-2 years):**
- Education at the top (until your first full-time job provides more signal)
- Projects section: 2-3 substantive projects with GitHub links, deployed URLs, and impact bullets. "Built a web app" is not a project description. "Built a recipe recommendation app with 200 daily users, using collaborative filtering on a 50K recipe dataset" is.
- Skills: languages, frameworks, tools — no ratings or proficiency levels (they are meaningless and look junior)
- Internships: treat them like jobs, use impact bullets

**Mid-level (2-7 years):**
- Experience at the top
- Education moves to the bottom
- Projects section optional (remove if experience section fills the page)
- Leadership signals matter: mention mentoring, design reviews, architecture decisions

**Senior/staff (7+ years):**
- Scope and influence signals are critical. Not just "built X" but "led the team that built X," "designed the architecture that 5 teams now use," "drove the technical decision to adopt Y across the org"
- System design contributions: "defined the data model for the new billing system," "authored the RFC for the microservice migration"
- Cross-functional impact: "partnered with product and data science to define the ML feature set"

## Keywords: The ATS Reality

Most large companies run resumes through ATS software that scores keyword matches against the job description before a human sees them. This is uncomfortable but real.

The solution is not to stuff your resume with keywords — it is to make sure your resume accurately reflects relevant experience using the same terminology the job description uses.

If the job description says "distributed systems" and your resume says "scalable backend services," you may not match. Both describe the same work. Use their vocabulary.

Scan job descriptions for technology names and include the ones you actually have experience with: specific languages, frameworks, databases, cloud platforms, and domain terms (e.g., "event-driven architecture," "REST APIs," "CI/CD").

## What to Remove

**Remove:**
- Objective statements ("seeking a challenging role where I can apply my skills...")
- References available upon request (assumed)
- Photos (outside certain European countries)
- Proficiency ratings for languages ("Python: 9/10")
- Unrelated jobs (if you have relevant experience, drop the barista job from 8 years ago)
- Graduation years older than 10-15 years (prevents age discrimination, adds no value)

**Remove carefully:**
- GPA: include if above 3.5 and you graduated within the last 3 years; remove otherwise
- Hobbies: only if they provide signal (e.g., competitive programming, open source contributions, relevant side projects)

## The One-Page Rule

For engineers under 10 years of experience, one page is the strong default. The reason is not arbitrary — it is that most resumes are scanned, not read, and a scanner covers more ground on one focused page than two diluted pages. If you are squeezing to fit one page, cut the oldest jobs, the least impactful bullets, and the soft-skill language first.

For 10+ years: two pages is acceptable. Three pages is not.

## Tailoring: How Much Is Enough?

Tailoring every resume for every job is not realistic when applying at scale. The pragmatic approach:

- Maintain a "master resume" with all your experience
- For each application, adjust the top 3-5 bullets under your most recent role to match the job description's priorities
- For roles that are a stretch (more senior, different domain), write a new top bullet that directly addresses the skill gap

Ten minutes of tailoring per application beats thirty minutes — but zero is worse than either.
