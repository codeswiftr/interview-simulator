# The Complete Onsite Interview Preparation Guide

Onsite interviews are different from phone screens in ways that go beyond format. You are being evaluated continuously — from how you greet the receptionist to how you handle a technical question you genuinely cannot answer. The preparation that matters is different too: less about cramming algorithms and more about managing the full-day cognitive and social load.

## The Week Before

**Five days out**: Stop learning new concepts. If you haven't internalized a topic by now, cramming it creates interference without adding fluency. Focus on review and practice. Do 2-3 coding problems per day that you have done before — the goal is to make good problem-solving habits automatic, not to expand your knowledge base.

**Three days out**: Research the company specifically. Read their engineering blog (last 6 months). Read their recent press (product launches, strategic moves). Look up your interviewers on LinkedIn — note their tenure, past companies, and any public writing. You are not doing this to impress them; you are doing it to have real things to say when asked "do you have any questions for me?"

**One day before**: Do nothing intensive. Light review at most. Get your logistics sorted: route to the office, parking, arrival time (aim for 10 minutes early), what to bring. Lay out your clothes. Charge your laptop if you'll need it. The one-day-before goal is to arrive tomorrow with low cortisol, not high preparation.

## Day Of

**Morning**: Eat a real breakfast. Avoid caffeine above your usual intake — interview anxiety combined with extra caffeine is a bad combination. Allow more transit time than you think you need; being rushed raises cortisol significantly.

**What to bring**: A notebook and pen (for talking through problems, taking notes during design interviews, and looking engaged during behavioral rounds). Water. A copy of your resume. Nothing else you need to manage.

**Arriving**: Announce your name clearly at reception. Greet your escort warmly but briefly. If offered water or coffee, accept water — it is useful, and it gives you something to do with your hands during pauses.

## Managing Back-to-Back Rounds

Most onsites have 4-6 consecutive rounds with 5-10 minute breaks. The energy management challenge is real.

**Between rounds**: Use the break to reset, not to review. Close your mental tab on the previous interview entirely — nothing you do in the next 5 minutes will change what just happened. Take three slow breaths. Drink water. Stand up and walk if you can.

**Energy dips**: The third or fourth round is typically when energy fades. Two things help: physical movement (stand briefly if there's a moment) and orienting fully toward the next interviewer. Curiosity is energizing — be genuinely interested in the next person.

**Tracking what you said**: Do not try to remember your answers across rounds for consistency. Different interviewers often ask similar questions. Just answer each question honestly and completely; worrying about what you told someone earlier is a distraction.

## When You Get Stuck in a Coding Round

Getting stuck is not failure. Every experienced interviewer has seen candidates get stuck; they are evaluating how you handle it, not just whether you get it.

**First 30 seconds of being stuck**: Keep talking. "I'm thinking about whether a hash map approach would work here, but I'm trying to figure out what the key should be..." Silence for more than 30-45 seconds reads as paralysis, even if you are thinking.

**After a minute of making no progress**: Ask a targeted question. Not "can you give me a hint?" — that's vague. Instead: "I'm stuck on how to handle duplicate entries in this approach. Is the input guaranteed to have unique values?" Asking a specific question signals structured thinking even when you are stuck.

**If you need a completely different approach**: Say so explicitly. "I don't think this direction is working. Let me step back and try a different angle." Pivoting confidently is better than grinding on a dead end.

**If you truly cannot solve it**: Describe what you would do to solve it, even if you cannot implement it. "I believe this is a dynamic programming problem where the state is X and the transition is Y — I'm struggling with the exact recurrence, but here's how I'd think through it." Partial credit for structured thinking is real.

## The Debrief / Closing Conversation

Many onsites end with a recruiter meeting or a closing conversation with a hiring manager. This is not a formality — it is your chance to express genuine interest and ask the questions that will help you make a decision if you get an offer.

**Expressing interest**: Be direct without being sycophantic. "I found the conversation with [name] about [technical problem] genuinely interesting — that kind of problem is exactly what I want to be working on." Specific > generic.

**Questions to ask that reveal real information**:
- "What does the technical mentorship path look like for someone joining at my level?"
- "What's a project that shipped in the last 6 months that the team is proud of, and what was hard about it?"
- "What do engineers here typically wish they'd known before joining?"
- "Where is the team struggling right now?"

These questions get real answers. "What's the company culture like?" does not.

## Post-Onsite

Send a brief thank-you email to the recruiter within 24 hours. Not to each interviewer individually — to the recruiter to pass along. Keep it short: what you appreciated about the day, one specific technical topic you found genuinely interesting, and that you look forward to hearing back. Three sentences is enough.

The most important post-onsite action is the debrief you do for yourself: what went well, what you would do differently, what technical topics you need to study. This debrief is how you get better at interviews, independent of the outcome.
