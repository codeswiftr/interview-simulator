# Best Online Courses and Resources for Software Engineering Interview Prep

The market for interview prep resources is enormous and mostly mediocre. Most candidates waste time on resources that feel productive but don't move the needle on actual interview performance. This guide cuts through the noise: what resources are actually worth your time, for which type of preparation, and how to structure them into an effective study plan.

## The Honest Assessment of LeetCode

LeetCode is both necessary and insufficient. It's necessary because the algorithmic interview format — implement a solution to a coding problem in 20-45 minutes — is standard at most large tech companies, and LeetCode is the most comprehensive database of problems similar to what you'll encounter.

It's insufficient because LeetCode alone doesn't teach you how to interview: how to communicate your thinking, how to handle hints from an interviewer, how to structure your solution before coding, how to test your code out loud. These skills require practice in a format closer to the real interview.

The effective LeetCode approach: use the Neetcode 150 roadmap (free, at neetcode.io) rather than random problem grinding. The 150 problems are organized by pattern (sliding window, two pointers, trees, graphs, dynamic programming) and cover the highest-frequency categories efficiently. Doing 150 curated problems with genuine understanding beats 400 random problems.

## Platforms Worth Using

**LeetCode** (leetcode.com): The standard. The Premium subscription ($35/month) adds company-specific problem lists and frequency data. Worth it for 2-3 months before a specific company's interview — the company tags are directionally accurate.

**Neetcode.io**: Free video explanations for every problem in the Neetcode 150. Clearer than LeetCode's own editorial solutions, with time/space complexity analysis. Start here if you're confused after reading a LeetCode editorial.

**AlgoExpert** ($99 one-time): Video explanations, language support, and a structured path. Better production quality than Neetcode but less comprehensive. Worth it if you learn better from polished video content.

**Interviewing.io**: Anonymous mock interviews with senior engineers from top companies. The feedback is calibrated to real interview standards because the interviewers are real engineers. Expensive ($225-300/session) but uniquely valuable — you can't replicate this with a friend doing a mock interview.

**Pramp** (pramp.com): Free peer-to-peer mock interviews. The quality varies (you're practicing with other candidates, not senior engineers), but the format practice is valuable. Do 5-10 Pramp sessions before a major interview loop.

**Educative.io**: The "Grokking the Coding Interview" course is popular for pattern-based learning. Worth it if pattern-based study clicks for you over problem-based study.

## For System Design Specifically

System design preparation requires different resources:

**"System Design Interview" by Alex Xu** (Books 1 and 2): The de facto standard. Not perfectly rigorous but gives you a vocabulary and framework for discussing common systems (URL shorteners, rate limiters, distributed key-value stores). Read this before doing system design mock interviews.

**ByteByteGo** (bytebytego.com): Alex Xu's companion platform with animated explanations of system concepts. Subscription is $79/year. The visual format makes concepts like consistent hashing and CDN architecture click faster than text.

**High Scalability blog** (highscalability.com): Real system architecture teardowns of production systems at scale. Reading these regularly builds intuition for what real systems look like — more grounded than textbook exercises.

**GitHub: system-design-primer**: Free, comprehensive, and covers the major categories. Less structured than Xu's book but more depth in some areas. Good supplement, not a replacement.

## For Behavioral Interviews

The resources for behavioral interviews are shorter and simpler:

**The STAR framework**: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Write out 10-15 STAR stories covering: a challenging project, a conflict with a colleague, a technical decision you made and its outcome, a time you failed and what you learned, a time you influenced without authority. This is all you need.

**Company-specific values/principles**: Amazon's Leadership Principles are the most famous and most tested. Read them, understand what each means, and prepare STAR examples that map to them. Microsoft, Apple, and Google also have stated values that behavioral interviewers reference.

## The Honest Study Plan

For most candidates with 8-12 weeks to prepare:

**Weeks 1-4**: Neetcode 150 patterns. Focus: arrays/hashing, two pointers, sliding window, stack, binary search. Do 2-3 problems per day, write down your approach before coding, analyze time/space complexity for every solution.

**Weeks 5-7**: Trees, graphs, DP. These are harder and higher-variance in interviews. If DP consistently stumps you, deprioritize it — spend the time on trees and graphs, which are higher frequency.

**Weeks 8-10**: System design. Read Alex Xu's book, watch ByteByteGo videos for the systems you find confusing, do one mock system design interview per week.

**Weeks 11-12**: Mock interviews, behavioral preparation, company-specific research.

The resource trap: accumulating more study materials feels like progress but isn't. One well-used resource is worth ten barely-started courses. Commit to one path and finish it.

## What to Stop Doing

Stop grinding LeetCode hard problems before you can consistently solve mediums. Hard problems in interviews are rare; the ability to execute mediums cleanly under time pressure is what determines most outcomes.

Stop using resources that don't require you to produce output. Watching video solutions without attempting the problem first is passive — you learn less than you think. Force yourself to attempt every problem before watching the explanation.

Stop trying to cover everything. If the interview is in 4 weeks and you haven't started dynamic programming, deprioritize it. Solid performance on high-frequency patterns beats shaky coverage of everything.

The best interview preparation is mostly unglamorous: solving problems, writing out solutions, reviewing your wrong answers, and doing mock interviews until the format feels familiar. The resources above are tools that support that process — they don't replace it.
