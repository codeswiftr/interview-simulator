Most interview prep advice assumes you're gunning for Google or Meta. That framing has shaped an entire industry of LeetCode grind culture, algorithm flashcards, and system design runbooks built for services operating at planetary scale. It's not wrong advice — for those companies. But if you're also considering startups, or if you're trying to keep your options open, you need to understand that the interview game shifts dramatically depending on where you're applying.

The underlying question being asked is different. At a big tech company, the question is "can this person reason about hard problems under structured constraints?" At a startup, the question is closer to "can this person help us ship the thing we need to ship next month?"

## What Startup Interviews Actually Test

Startup interviews are less standardized, which makes them harder to predict — but easier to prepare for if you understand the intent. A startup isn't trying to sort you into a percentile. They're trying to answer a concrete question: can you own an end-to-end slice of the product?

This means you'll encounter more take-home assignments, more open-ended coding sessions where you're asked to build a small feature rather than solve a contrived puzzle, and more conversations about how you've navigated ambiguity in past roles. The founder or lead engineer interviewing you is often thinking, "Would I trust this person to figure out something I don't have time to specify perfectly?" That's a very different bar than algorithmic correctness.

Breadth matters more than depth here. Startups need generalists who can touch the database, the API layer, and the frontend in the same week without needing a team around them. If you've only ever worked deep in one layer of a large system, that's worth knowing before you walk in.

Product thinking is also legitimately tested, not just as a checkbox. You may be asked how you'd approach a feature, what tradeoffs you'd make, or what you'd cut to hit a deadline. These aren't trick questions — they're trying to see whether you understand why the software exists, not just how it works.

### The Bar Shifts by Stage

A 5-person Series A startup and a 300-person Series C company will interview very differently. At the early stage, you're often talking directly to the founders, the process is informal, and they're hiring for trust and energy as much as technical skill. The take-home might be something scraped together the night before. Be prepared for that ambiguity — it's a feature, not a bug.

By Series B, the company has started to institutionalize. There's a technical lead or VP of Engineering shaping the process. You'll see more structured technical interviews, perhaps a system design round that wasn't there before, and more behavioral questions tied to the company's stated values. The bar for engineering rigor goes up because the cost of a bad hire has become visible to them.

Late-stage startups — 200+ people, thinking about an IPO — often borrow heavily from big tech playbooks. Don't be surprised to see a full algorithmic coding round, a system design session, and a behavioral loop that mirrors what you'd find at a much larger company.

## How Big Tech (FAANG+) Interviews Differ

The big tech interview process is highly legible if you know what to expect, and brutal if you don't. The algorithmic coding round is not testing whether you can Google a solution — it's testing whether you have enough fluency with data structures, graph traversal, dynamic programming, and complexity analysis to reason through novel problems in real time. LeetCode hard is the target difficulty, not the ceiling.

System design rounds at this level are explicitly about scale. "Design a URL shortener" is a warmup question to see whether you understand consistent hashing, replication lag, and cache invalidation before you get to the real ask. These companies run systems that serve hundreds of millions of users, and they want evidence that you can think about failure modes, load distribution, and data consistency at that level — even if your day-to-day work won't require it immediately.

Behavioral interviews at the largest tech companies are often anchored to formal leadership principles or competency frameworks. At Amazon, that means STAR-format answers tied explicitly to their Leadership Principles. The behavior they're probing for is consistent: ownership, bias for action, and the ability to disagree and commit. Know the framework before you sit down.

## Preparing for Both Simultaneously

The good news is there's significant overlap. If you build a solid foundation, you're covering most of what both types of companies care about, and you can layer on the specifics.

The 80/20 approach works like this: spend your preparation time on algorithms and data structures (covering LeetCode medium fluently, LeetCode hard occasionally), system design fundamentals (databases, caching, queuing, APIs, tradeoffs), and building a concrete portfolio of projects you can talk about at depth. That core preparation is transferable almost everywhere.

What you add on top of that foundation depends on where you're interviewing. For big tech, extend the algorithms work to hard problems and practice system design at scale. For startups, invest time in articulating your product thinking, preparing stories about ambiguous situations you navigated well, and being able to speak credibly about end-to-end ownership of something real.

### The Fit Problem

One thing that often goes undiscussed: there's a real mismatch risk that has nothing to do with your technical ability.

An engineer who has spent years optimizing for FAANG interviews — grinding algorithms, studying distributed systems theory, perfecting behavioral answers — can genuinely struggle in an early startup interview. The startup interviewer isn't looking for someone who can reverse a linked list in O(n); they're looking for someone who's going to pick up a messy codebase with minimal documentation and ship a feature before the customer churn gets worse. If your entire preparation has been abstract and theoretical, that context-switching can be jarring, and it shows.

The reverse is equally true. A scrappy startup engineer who's shipped a lot but hasn't touched algorithmic problem-solving in years will likely find the big tech process punishing, regardless of how good they are at their actual job.

Neither situation reflects a failure of engineering ability. They reflect a mismatch between what a process is testing and what a candidate has optimized for. Knowing which game you're playing — and at which stage of preparation you're in — is the most underrated part of job searching.

The best move is to be honest with yourself about where you are and where you want to go, then build your preparation around the gap rather than the parts you're already strong in.
