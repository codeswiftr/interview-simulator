# How to Build a Portfolio That Gets Interviews

Most engineering portfolios fail not because the engineer lacks skill, but because the portfolio signals the wrong things to the people evaluating it. A beautiful portfolio site doesn't help if hiring managers can't tell what kind of engineer you are in 90 seconds. This guide covers what actually works.

## Who Looks at Engineering Portfolios and What They're Scanning For

Portfolio reviewers at tech companies are usually:
- **Hiring managers** who skim for signal about the type of engineer you are (systems thinker, product-focused, tooling-oriented) and whether your experience maps to what their team needs
- **Recruiters** who check for flags and confirm basic qualifications before advancing a candidate
- **Senior engineers** reviewing take-home assessments or code samples alongside application materials

All three groups are time-constrained. The question they're implicitly asking in the first 90 seconds: "Is there a signal here that this person can do what we need?"

The failure mode: portfolios that require too much exploration to find that signal. Too many projects, no clear focus, every project at the same "built this at a hackathon" level.

## GitHub Profile: The Actual Portfolio

For most working engineers, the GitHub profile matters more than any personal portfolio site. Hiring managers who look at GitHub are evaluating:

**Commit history breadth and consistency**: Not about gaming the contribution graph — about whether your commit messages are professional, your commit frequency suggests ongoing engagement, and your projects show maintenance (not just creation). A project with commits only during two weeks in 2022 signals it was never finished.

**Code quality in public repos**: Hiring managers and engineers will read your code. The question: does this person write code the way we expect engineers at our company to write code? Readability matters more than cleverness. A README that clearly explains what the project does, how to run it, and why you built it matters a lot.

**What you've contributed to others' repos**: Open-source contributions — even small ones — signal that you can read other people's code and contribute constructively. A merged PR on a meaningful project is worth more on a portfolio than most solo projects.

**The projects you choose to make public**: Your pinned projects are your signal. Pin projects that show the specific kind of engineering you want to do next, not your oldest or most sentimental work.

## What Makes a Portfolio Project Land

The most common portfolio project failure: building what's easy to build rather than what demonstrates relevant skill. "A to-do app" or "a weather app" signals that you can follow a tutorial. Here's what actually works:

**Solve a real problem you had**: "I built this because I needed X and nothing existed that did Y" is more compelling than "I built this to learn React." The genuine motivation comes through in the description and the care put into edge cases.

**Use real infrastructure**: A project that runs on a real domain, uses a real database, handles real authentication, and has real error handling demonstrates production readiness. Localhost demos that require setup don't.

**Show the engineering decisions**: A README or blog post that explains why you chose PostgreSQL over MongoDB, or why you structured the API a particular way, is evidence of engineering judgment. Most portfolios skip this and leave reviewers to guess.

**Depth over breadth**: One project with real complexity (database schema, API design, auth, deployment, meaningful features) is better than six projects that each demonstrate a single framework. Companies hire engineers to build systems, not to demonstrate familiarity with libraries.

## The Portfolio Site Question

A personal portfolio site (yourname.dev, johnsmith.io) is useful for one specific purpose: a single URL you can put on your resume that links everything together — GitHub, blog, projects, contact. It doesn't need to be impressive on its own.

What to put on a portfolio site:
- A brief, specific description of what kind of engineer you are (not "full-stack developer" — something specific: "backend engineer specializing in data pipeline infrastructure")
- Links to 2-3 projects with one-paragraph descriptions of what each does and why you built it
- Link to your GitHub, any technical writing you've done, and contact information

What not to do: skill bar graphics ("JavaScript: 90%, Python: 75%"), skill lists that include every technology you've touched, project demos that are offline or broken. These signal either self-unawareness or carelessness.

## Technical Writing as Portfolio Content

Engineers who write publicly have an outsized advantage in hiring pipelines. A blog post, a technical tweet thread, or a detailed GitHub issue comment that demonstrates clear thinking, specific knowledge, and good communication signals hiring managers in ways that project lists can't.

You don't need to post frequently. Two or three genuinely good posts per year — a post-mortem on a project problem you solved, an explanation of a system you built, a comparison of tools you evaluated — are worth more than 50 brief posts.

The value compounds: technical writing becomes an SEO-discoverability mechanism for your profile, a portfolio of evidence for promotion discussions, and practice for the technical communication that engineers need in every meeting.

## Targeting Your Portfolio to the Role

The right portfolio for a startup that wants a generalist is different from the right portfolio for a FAANG team that wants a specialist. Before investing portfolio effort:

For startups: show that you can ship end-to-end. Deployed projects that solve real problems. Breadth across the stack demonstrates the "wear many hats" quality they need.

For large companies: show depth in the relevant domain. If you want to join a data infrastructure team, projects or blog posts about distributed data systems matter more than your front-end work.

For FAANG: the portfolio matters less than the algorithmic interview process. But the portfolio that helps here is open-source contributions and blog posts that demonstrate systems thinking — the qualities that correlate with doing well in their interviews.

The most efficient portfolio investment: whatever you're going to build anyway for work or learning, make it public. Write one sentence about each project that explains why it matters. Link everything from a central profile. That's it — that's enough.
