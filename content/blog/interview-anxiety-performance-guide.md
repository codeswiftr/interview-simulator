# Managing Interview Anxiety and Performing Under Pressure

Technical interview anxiety is nearly universal among software engineers, and it's a performance problem — not a knowledge problem. Engineers who solve algorithm problems effortlessly at home blank in live interviews. They know the material. The anxiety is what breaks performance. This guide covers what actually works to manage interview anxiety and perform consistently.

## Understanding What's Happening Physiologically

Anxiety isn't just mental — it's a physiological state. When you perceive a threat (negative evaluation, failure, rejection), your sympathetic nervous system activates: heart rate increases, cortisol and adrenaline release, blood flow prioritizes large muscle groups over the prefrontal cortex (the part you need for solving problems).

This is the "threat" vs. "challenge" distinction from sports psychology research. The same physiological arousal can be experienced as threatening ("I might fail") or as challenge ("I'm ready for this"). High-performers learn to reframe the arousal.

The implication: trying to eliminate the anxiety is the wrong goal. Managing and reframing it is the right goal.

## Pre-Interview Preparation That Reduces Anxiety

Anxiety is higher when uncertainty is higher. Reducing uncertainty reduces anxiety.

**Know the format in detail**: How long is the interview? What type (live coding, take-home, system design)? What platform? Will there be one interviewer or two? Who are they (LinkedIn their background)? Knowing exactly what's coming reduces the threat response.

**Practice in the interview format**: If you practice on LeetCode but interview in CoderPad, you've added unnecessary friction. Practice in the actual format — time yourself, use the same editor, say your thinking out loud (not just in your head). Mock interviews are more valuable than solo practice for this reason.

**Reduce novelty**: Do a dry run — open the interview platform the day before, test your audio/video setup, confirm your internet connection works, prepare your space. Friction on logistics the day of amplifies stress. Remove it.

**Physical state matters**: Sleep is the highest-leverage variable for cognitive performance. A well-rested brain on 8 hours outperforms an anxious brain on 5 hours regardless of preparation. Schedule interviews for your peak performance time if you have a choice (most people perform better mid-morning than immediately after waking).

## The 5 Minutes Before the Interview

The pre-interview window has an outsized effect on performance.

**Avoid cramming**: Reviewing difficult problems immediately before an interview increases anxiety without improving recall. The material you know is the material you know. Reviewing in the 30 minutes before an interview is counterproductive.

**Physical activation**: A 5-minute walk, standing and moving, or power posing (standing tall, expansive posture) measurably affects cortisol/testosterone balance. This sounds like pseudo-science but has replicable research behind it. At minimum, don't sit hunched over your laptop reviewing notes — the body posture affects mental state.

**Reframe the arousal**: Say to yourself (or out loud, alone): "I'm excited." Not "calm down" — trying to suppress arousal backfires. Reframing nervousness as excitement uses the same physiological state more productively. Research from Harvard Business School supports this.

**Recall a specific success**: Spend 2 minutes recalling a specific interview, presentation, or technical moment that went well. In detail — what you were wearing, the room, what you said, the positive response. This activates performance-associated memories and sets a confident baseline.

## In-Interview Techniques

**Slow down deliberately**: Anxiety accelerates — you talk faster, think faster, skip steps. The antidote is deliberate slowing. Read the problem twice before saying anything. Take a breath before responding to a follow-up question. Silence for 2-3 seconds feels like eternity to you; to the interviewer, it signals thoughtfulness.

**Use the clarification stage as a reset**: The first 5 minutes of a coding interview are clarification time. Use them fully, even if you think you understand the problem. Ask at least two clarifying questions. This serves double duty: getting information you might need, and giving yourself time to calm and think.

**Name the process**: When you're stuck, narrate: "I know I need to find a path through this graph. I'm thinking about whether BFS or DFS is more appropriate here..." This keeps the interviewer engaged, buys you time to think, and externally processes the problem — which often leads to solutions.

**Accept that mistakes happen**: The goal of a technical interview is not a perfect performance. You are allowed to make mistakes, correct them, and keep going. Interviewers who've done hundreds of interviews know what strong performance looks like — it includes making and recovering from mistakes. What they're watching is how you recover, not whether you're perfect.

## When You Blank Completely

Blanking — where you stare at a problem and feel your mind empty — is common. Here's the protocol:

1. State what you're doing: "I'm going to take a moment to think about this." Silence you've named is okay; unannounced silence is awkward.
2. Write out what you know: Write the function signature, the input type, the output type. Sometimes kinesthetic activity (physically writing) breaks the freeze.
3. Work a small example manually: "If the input is [1, 2, 3], the expected output is 6. So I need to..." Concrete examples unlock pattern recognition when abstract thinking is blocked.
4. Ask for a hint: "I'm stuck on how to approach the optimization. Could you point me in a direction?" Most interviewers will help if you ask directly.

## After the Interview

Debriefing immediately after each interview — what went well, what you blanked on, what you'd approach differently — accelerates improvement more than doing additional practice problems. The interview is data. Treat it as such.

Don't ruminate on mistakes between interviews in a loop. Note them for debrief, then consciously set them aside. Mental bandwidth spent on past interviews is unavailable for current preparation.

The engineers who improve fastest at interviewing treat it as a skill with a feedback loop, not a fixed performance that reflects their worth. The separation between preparation and performance, between knowledge and anxiety management, is what consistent interviewers understand and apply.
