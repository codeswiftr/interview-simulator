# Software Engineer Salary Transparency Guide: Knowing Your Worth in 2026

Salary transparency has transformed software engineering compensation over the past decade. Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Blind, and legislative salary transparency laws have made compensation data more accessible than ever. Yet many engineers still underearn because they don't know how to use this information effectively or they're uncomfortable with the negotiation process. This guide demystifies engineering compensation.

## Understanding Total Compensation Components

"Salary" undersells the full picture of engineering compensation. Total compensation (TC) includes:

**Base salary**: Fixed annual cash, paid in regular payroll installments. Tax-advantaged in straightforward ways. The most comparable number across companies.

**Equity (RSUs or options)**: At public companies, RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) vest over time and are worth the market value of the shares at vest. At startups, options have a strike price — they're only worth something if the company's stock price exceeds that strike price. RSU value is predictable; option value is speculative.

**Annual bonus**: Discretionary (at most companies) based on individual and company performance. Finance and gaming companies often have meaningful cash bonuses (20-30% of base); most tech companies have smaller bonuses (5-15%) or none.

**Signing bonus**: One-time payment, often used to offset unvested equity at a previous employer. May have clawback provisions (you repay if you leave within 12-24 months).

**Benefits**: Health insurance quality varies dramatically. 401k match (effectively free compensation — always maximize), employee stock purchase plans (ESPPs), and generous parental leave add measurable value.

**Refresher grants**: After initial RSU grant vests, most companies grant additional equity. This is often negotiated at offer and determines long-term comp trajectory more than the initial grant.

At FAANG and top-tier tech companies, equity often exceeds base salary at senior levels. Comparing only base salaries is meaningless at these companies.

## Using Compensation Data Sources Effectively

**Levels.fyi**: The most accurate database for public tech companies. Self-reported but with strong data integrity (verified offer letters at some companies). Use it to find:
- Median and range for your target level at specific companies
- How your current compensation compares to peers
- Trend data (compensation changes over time)

**Glassdoor**: Broader company coverage including non-tech companies and smaller firms. Less detailed than Levels.fyi for tech roles. Useful for companies not covered by Levels.fyi.

**Blind**: Anonymous software engineering forum with active compensation discussion. Useful for real-time market signal and company-specific gossip that doesn't appear in formal data.

**LinkedIn Salary Insights**: Useful for broad market context, especially for non-FAANG roles and regional comparisons.

**Recruiter conversations**: Recruiters know the market better than anyone. Asking "What's the budgeted range for this role?" in early conversations yields useful signal and is completely normal.

## When and How to Discuss Compensation

**With the recruiter (screening stage)**: Establish the range early. "Before we go further, I want to make sure the comp range is in the right ballpark — I'm targeting $X-$Y total comp. Is that within your budget?" This saves everyone time. If they won't share a number, give yours first.

**Never first in negotiation**: Don't volunteer your current salary or your minimum acceptable number. "I'm focused on finding the right role — I'm confident we can find something that works if the fit is right." Many states now prohibit companies from asking for salary history.

**After the offer**: The offer is the beginning of negotiation, not the end. "I'm really excited about this opportunity. The offer is [slightly below] what I was expecting based on my research. Is there flexibility on the base/equity/signing bonus?" Companies expect this.

**Competing offers**: A real competing offer is the most powerful negotiation leverage. "I've received an offer from [Company B] for $X total comp. I'd prefer to join [Company A] — is there anything you can do to close the gap?"

## Common Compensation Mistakes

**Anchoring to your current salary**: Your value isn't what your current employer pays you — it's what the market pays for your skills. Underpaid engineers who accept 15% raises are still underpaid.

**Ignoring equity**: An offer with $30K lower base but $100K more equity over 4 years (at a stable public company) is a better offer. Do the math on total comp, not just base.

**Failing to negotiate**: Studies consistently show negotiation improves outcomes for almost everyone who tries it. Companies expect negotiation. "The offer is lower than I expected" is sufficient — you don't need a lengthy justification.

**Accepting the first response**: "The offer is fixed" or "We don't have flexibility" is almost never literally true. It means "we'd prefer not to move." A polite second ask often yields a different response.

**Not considering level calibration**: If you're offered a lower level than you interviewed for, it usually means lower comp with a "fast path to promotion" promise. Negotiate the level first, then the comp. Level at top tech companies dramatically affects long-term earning trajectory.

## Negotiation Scripts That Work

Counteroffer: "Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about the role and the team. Based on my research and comparable offers I've seen, I was expecting [X]. Is there flexibility to get closer to that?"

Competing offer: "I've received an offer from [Company] at [TC amount]. I'm very interested in joining [Company A] — can you help me close that gap?"

Equity for base tradeoff: "I'm flexible on the mix — would it be possible to adjust the equity grant to bring total comp to [target]?"

The engineering compensation market rewards those who treat compensation as part of the professional relationship, not as a personal confrontation. Recruiters and hiring managers negotiate regularly — they respect candidates who know their value and advocate for it professionally.
