# Interview Nerves and Performance: How to Manage Anxiety and Perform Under Pressure

Most guides treat interview nerves as a weakness to eliminate. They're not. Moderate anxiety activates performance-enhancing arousal — it makes you more alert, more focused, and more engaged. The goal isn't to eliminate nerves; it's to keep them in the zone where they enhance rather than impair performance.

## Understanding Why Interviews Are Uniquely Stressful

Technical interviews trigger multiple anxiety sources simultaneously: performance evaluation, social judgment, time pressure, and high stakes. Understanding these helps you target your preparation.

**Performance evaluation anxiety:** The fear of being judged as incompetent. Reduces by: demonstrating genuine competence through preparation. There's no psychological shortcut here — nothing reduces performance anxiety as effectively as actually being prepared.

**Social judgment anxiety:** Fear of being unlikeable or making a poor personal impression. Reduces by: shifting focus from "what do they think of me" to "what can I learn about them?" Curiosity and genuine engagement replace self-consciousness.

**Time pressure anxiety:** The ticking clock that makes simple problems seem impossible. Reduces by: practicing under time pressure in preparation so the artificial clock feels familiar.

**Stakes anxiety:** The weight of "everything is riding on this." Reduces by: distributing the stakes — never having one interview that "decides everything." Pipeline of multiple opportunities deflates any single interview's power.

## Physiological Regulation

Your body's stress response (elevated heart rate, shallow breathing, tight muscles) is triggered by threat appraisal. You can interrupt it physiologically:

**Box breathing (4-7-8 or 4-4-4-4):** Inhale 4 counts, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces cortisol within minutes. Practice this before you log in to a video interview — 5 cycles in the bathroom or car.

**Power posture:** Two minutes in an expansive posture (hands on hips, standing tall, open chest) before a high-stakes situation has documented effects on confidence and cortisol. Not a solution alone, but a useful tool.

**Temperature:** Cold water on the wrists (pulse points) rapidly reduces arousal. Warm water on hands reduces anxiety. Small environmental controls with measurable effect.

**Exercise same day:** 20-30 minutes of moderate cardio on interview day reduces baseline cortisol and improves cognitive performance. Not a run right before — earlier in the day.

## Mental Reframing

**"Challenge" vs. "Threat" framing:** The same physiological arousal state is interpreted as performance-enhancing or performance-impairing depending on your cognitive appraisal. Tell yourself: "I'm excited about this challenge" instead of "I'm anxious about this threat." This isn't self-deception — you're choosing the more accurate framing. An interview is an opportunity, not a threat.

**Perspective anchoring:** What's the worst realistic outcome? Rejection. You continue your current job or job search. Life continues. Catastrophic thinking exaggerates stakes; real outcomes are usually survivable. This isn't dismissiveness — it's accurate calibration.

**Process focus vs. outcome focus:** Focus on doing each step of the interview well, not on whether you'll get an offer. "I will explain my approach clearly" is within your control. "I will get an offer" is not. Process focus reduces anxiety because you're focused on the doable.

## When You Freeze Mid-Interview

Freezing (going blank under pressure) is a normal anxiety response. Having a script for it prevents it from spiraling:

**Acknowledge it:** "I'm drawing a blank on that — let me think through it more carefully." This is better than silence or visibly panicking. Most interviewers respond with patience.

**Return to first principles:** If you've forgotten a specific fact or approach, reason from what you know: "I don't remember the exact complexity, but I know a hash map lookup is O(1) and we're doing n lookups, so the total should be O(n)."

**Ask for a moment:** "Can I take 30 seconds to think through this?" This is entirely acceptable. Interviewers prefer a clear organized answer after a pause to a confused stream of consciousness.

**Redirect to what you know:** "I'm not sure about that specific approach, but I do know that [related concept]. Could we explore that angle?" This is honest and moves the conversation forward.

## Preparation as the Foundation

All the anxiety management techniques above are supplements, not foundations. The deepest anxiety reduction comes from being genuinely prepared:

- You've solved this problem type before, in practice
- You know your own experience well enough to answer any behavioral question
- You've done enough mock interviews that the format is familiar, not novel

Preparation converts "I hope I know what to do" to "I know what to do." The remaining anxiety is performance arousal — which, managed well, is an asset.

**Rehearse out loud:** Silent practice doesn't prepare you for spoken performance. Practice answering questions out loud, narrating your code, and explaining your design decisions verbally. The think-out-loud skill requires practice with actual speech, not mental rehearsal.

The engineers who perform best in interviews are rarely those with the lowest anxiety — they're those who've developed a functional relationship with their anxiety. They feel it, accept it as part of the experience, and perform through it.
