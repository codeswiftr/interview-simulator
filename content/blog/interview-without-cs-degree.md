# How to Get a Software Engineering Job Without a CS Degree

The degree question comes up constantly in engineering hiring, and the honest answer is more nuanced than most advice acknowledges. Some companies care deeply. Others have explicitly removed the requirement. Most fall somewhere in the middle, where the degree is a proxy signal that you can replace with other evidence — if you know what to replace it with.

This is a playbook for non-traditional candidates: bootcamp graduates, self-taught engineers, career switchers, and anyone who learned to code without a four-year CS program.

## The Actual Hiring Landscape

Large tech companies vary significantly in how they handle degree requirements. Google, Apple, and IBM have publicly stated that they do not require a CS degree. Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft have similar policies on paper, though individual recruiters and hiring managers still make judgment calls. Startups are generally more pragmatic — they care what you can build, and many founders did not take a traditional path either.

The tightest gatekeeping tends to happen at companies using automated resume screening, defense contractors with security clearance requirements, and large enterprises in regulated industries. If you are applying through an ATS system at a Fortune 500 company and your resume gets filtered before a human sees it, the degree field on the form may be the blocker.

The most accessible roles for non-traditional candidates are at companies that do their own technical screening early — where a coding assessment or take-home project replaces the resume filter. Also look at roles titled "software engineer" rather than "software developer" at smaller companies, and at companies that are actively hiring bootcamp graduates (many publish this openly).

## What Replaces the Degree Signal

A CS degree communicates a few things to a hiring team: you can learn hard technical material, you stuck with something for four years, and you have been exposed to foundational concepts like data structures, algorithms, and systems. Your application needs to communicate those same things through different evidence.

**Portfolio projects** are the highest-leverage signal for non-traditional candidates. One well-built project that solves a real problem and is deployed somewhere is worth more than a dozen tutorials. The project should have a public GitHub repo with a clean README that explains what it does, how to run it, and what technical decisions were made and why. If you built something that people actually use, even a small audience, lead with that.

**GitHub activity** shows that you write and ship code regularly. It does not need to be full of green squares every day, but a consistent history of commits across real projects is visible evidence that you code. Pinned repos on your profile should be your best work. Keep them maintained — broken CI and outdated dependencies signal abandonment.

**Bootcamp credentials** carry weight at companies that actively recruit from bootcamps. Name recognition matters here: General Assembly, App Academy, Hack Reactor, and similar programs have hiring networks. If you went through one of these, list it prominently. If you did an online program, focus less on the credential and more on what you built during it.

**Open source contributions** are particularly compelling because they prove you can read and work within an existing codebase — a core job skill that portfolio projects alone do not demonstrate. Even small contributions to established projects (bug fixes, documentation, test coverage) show that you can navigate unfamiliar code and collaborate with other engineers on a shared codebase.

## How to Prepare for the Coding Interview

The technical interview at most engineering roles follows a predictable structure: coding problems that test your knowledge of data structures and algorithms. You do not need a CS degree to learn this material. You do need to be deliberate about what you study and in what order.

Start with the fundamentals before you touch LeetCode. Make sure you understand arrays, strings, hash maps, and the basic mechanics of recursion. These cover the majority of easy and medium difficulty interview problems. Then add trees (binary trees, traversal patterns), basic graph concepts (BFS, DFS), and sorting algorithms with their time complexity.

The realistic study timeline for someone preparing seriously is three to four months of consistent practice — roughly one to two hours a day on most days. You will not cover everything. Focus coverage on the patterns that appear most frequently: two pointers, sliding window, binary search, depth-first search, and dynamic programming basics. The Blind 75 problem list is a reasonable starting point. NeetCode's organized roadmap is better if you want structure.

Practice explaining your thinking out loud from the beginning. The interview is as much about communication as it is about getting the right answer. Engineers who work through a problem verbally and collaborate with the interviewer perform better than engineers who code in silence and hand over a solution.

Do not neglect system design. At senior and above it is the focus, but even for mid-level roles a basic question about how you would architect a URL shortener or a rate limiter is common. You do not need deep expertise, but you should be able to sketch a reasonable architecture and explain the tradeoffs of your choices.

## How to Address "No Degree" Without Apologizing

The worst thing you can do in an interview or cover letter is lead with an apology or explanation about not having a degree. It signals insecurity and draws attention to something that might otherwise be a non-issue.

In a cover letter, do not mention the degree at all unless the job posting explicitly requires one. Lead with what you have built and what problems you have solved. If the posting says "CS degree or equivalent experience," you qualify by virtue of your portfolio and any professional experience you have.

In an interview, if the topic comes up, treat it as a neutral fact. "I am self-taught / I went through a bootcamp / I studied independently" delivered with confidence reads very differently than "I know I do not have a degree, but..." The second framing is asking the interviewer to overlook something. The first is simply stating your background.

The most effective way to neutralize the degree question is to make it irrelevant through demonstrated competence. If you solved the coding problems well, if your system design showed clear thinking, and if your portfolio includes shipped work, the degree question fades.

## The Companies and Roles to Target First

Some companies and roles are structurally more accessible for non-traditional candidates. Startups at the seed and Series A stage hire based on what you can ship, not credentials. Companies that publish that they hire bootcamp graduates are telling you they have a working pipeline for non-traditional engineers. Remote-first companies often have more meritocratic hiring because they have more applicants and rely more on structured technical assessments.

For roles, backend and full-stack web development tend to have more non-traditional engineers than systems programming, embedded systems, or compiler work — where deep CS fundamentals are genuinely required for the job. Mobile development, especially with React Native, and frontend roles with a strong JavaScript/TypeScript focus are also accessible entry points.

Your goal for the first job is to get experience. The second job becomes dramatically easier once you have a year or two of professional engineering on your resume. The degree question diminishes in importance with every year of professional experience you add. Most engineers with five or more years in the industry are evaluated almost entirely on what they have shipped, not how they got there.

The path is real. It takes longer to prepare without a structured program, and you will need to be more deliberate about what you study. But the technical interview does not ask for your transcript. It asks you to solve problems. Prepare for what is actually being tested, and you will be ready.
