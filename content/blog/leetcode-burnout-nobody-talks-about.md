# The LeetCode Burnout Nobody Talks About

*Why thousands of talented engineers are questioning their careers over arbitrary coding puzzles*

---

There's a confession thread that surfaces on Blind every few weeks. It starts the same way:

> "5 years of LeetCode with no progress. I'm giving up."

The poster describes years of grinding. Hundreds of problems solved. Mock interviews. Premium subscriptions. Courses. YouTube tutorials. And still—rejection after rejection.

What makes this post different from typical venting? The comments. Hundreds of engineers sharing the same story. Senior developers at Fortune 500 companies admitting they'd fail their own company's interview today.

**This isn't about individual failure. It's about a broken system that's burning out an entire profession.**

---

## The Dirty Secret of Technical Interviews

Here's something interviewers won't tell you: your performance in a 45-minute whiteboard session has almost nothing to do with your ability to build software.

One senior engineer put it bluntly:

> "How you perform in an interview is not a measure of your worth as a software engineer—it is a measure of your ability to do interviews. The two are related but not equal."

Think about that. Years of building products, shipping features, mentoring teammates, debugging production systems at 2 AM—none of it matters when you're standing at a whiteboard trying to remember the optimal solution to "Trapping Rain Water."

The engineers who pass these interviews aren't necessarily better engineers. They're better at a very specific skill: performing algorithms under artificial pressure.

---

## The Real Cost Nobody Calculates

When we talk about LeetCode prep, we focus on hours. But the real cost is deeper:

**Creative death.** One developer described it perfectly:

> "I wasn't struggling because I couldn't solve problems. I was struggling because I started dreading the whole ritual. My creativity felt drained. I stopped tinkering on side projects. Motivation dipped. I stopped coding for love and started coding just to pass a test."

**Imposter syndrome amplification.** You're a senior engineer at a major company. You build systems serving millions of users. But you can't solve a medium-difficulty graph problem in 25 minutes. What does that make you?

A fraud, apparently.

**Relationship strain.** Evenings spent grinding instead of with family. Weekends lost to practice problems. The guilt of choosing algorithm study over your kid's soccer game.

**Career doubt.** After enough rejections, talented engineers start questioning everything:
- "Maybe I'm not cut out for this."
- "Maybe I should have become an accountant."
- "Maybe everyone else is smarter than me."

---

## The System Design Trap

If algorithm grinding is purgatory, system design interviews are hell—especially for senior engineers.

Here's the cruel irony: the more senior you become, the harder interviews get, but your daily work prepares you less.

> "The reality is most developers don't get to design such systems in their daily jobs. And even if you're lucky to work at a company that runs large-scale, globally distributed systems, learning how they work on your own is not easy."

You're expected to design Twitter's architecture in 45 minutes. But at your actual job? You work on a specific service within a specific team. You've never seen the full architecture diagram. You've never made decisions at the Principal Engineer level.

The scope mismatch is absurd:

> "The scope of a typical System Design interview question ranges from one of a Principal Software Engineer to a CTO. While 1-2 engineers can easily implement most tasks we generally work on, the scope of a System Design interview question requires 10-1000 engineers to implement."

---

## The Anxiety Epidemic

Let's talk about what nobody admits in public: the panic attacks.

> "Many REALLY good engineers have some form of anxiety disorder, and the standard practice for an interview is to put them in a pressure cooker situation."

Engineers describe:
- Waking at 3 AM overthinking preparation
- Being "in tears" after failed interviews
- Physical symptoms before on-sites
- Depression spirals after rejections

This isn't weakness. This is what happens when you design a system that evaluates human worth through artificial stress tests.

---

## The Contradiction We Accept

Software engineering is one of the few professions where:

1. Your actual work performance doesn't determine if you can get hired
2. Years of experience can make interviews harder, not easier
3. The best practitioners often fail the entry exams
4. Everyone knows the system is broken, but nobody changes it

We've normalized a process that would be considered absurd in any other field:

- Would you hire a surgeon based on a timed anatomy quiz?
- Would you hire a lawyer based on how fast they can recite case law?
- Would you hire a chef based on their ability to describe a recipe without cooking?

Yet we hire engineers based on their ability to implement merge sort from memory while someone watches.

---

## What's Actually Being Tested

After years of observing this system, here's what technical interviews actually measure:

1. **Pattern recognition** — Have you seen this exact problem before?
2. **Performance under artificial pressure** — Can you think clearly while being judged?
3. **Specific communication style** — Do you narrate your thoughts the "right" way?
4. **Interview-specific preparation** — How many hours did you grind?

Notice what's missing:
- Ability to build maintainable systems
- Collaboration skills
- Debugging complex production issues
- Mentoring and leadership
- Actual problem-solving in real contexts

---

## The Questions Nobody Asks

Before you start another LeetCode session, consider:

**Are you preparing for the job or the interview?** These are increasingly different things.

**What's the opportunity cost?** Every hour grinding algorithms is an hour not spent building, learning relevant skills, or living your life.

**Is this company worth it?** If a company evaluates you through a process that has nothing to do with the actual work, what does that say about their culture?

**What would happen if you just... didn't?** Some companies are abandoning traditional technical interviews. Some are hiring based on actual work samples. Some value experience over puzzle performance.

---

## The Unspoken Truth

Here's what experienced engineers know but rarely say out loud:

The best job you'll ever have probably won't come from acing a LeetCode-style interview.

It will come from:
- A referral from someone who's worked with you
- A company that evaluates based on real work
- A conversation where you discuss actual problems you've solved
- A hiring manager who values demonstrated experience over puzzle performance

The engineers who build the best careers aren't necessarily the best at interviews. They're the ones who find ways to demonstrate their value outside the traditional process.

---

## A Different Perspective

What if we stopped treating interview prep as a necessary evil and started questioning whether it's necessary at all?

What if the goal isn't to get better at a broken game, but to find the games worth playing?

What if the engineers "failing" interviews aren't failures at all—they're just playing by rules that were never designed to measure what actually matters?

---

*This post doesn't have a happy ending or a 10-step solution. Sometimes the most important thing is acknowledging that the struggle is real, the system is broken, and you're not alone in feeling this way.*

*If you're in the middle of interview prep hell right now, know this: your worth as an engineer isn't determined by your ability to invert a binary tree on command. It never was.*
