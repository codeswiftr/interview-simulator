# LeetCode Burnout: The Silent Epidemic in Tech Interviews (And How to Cope)

*Why thousands of talented engineers are questioning their careers over arbitrary coding puzzles—and what we can do about it.*

---

There's a confession thread that surfaces on Blind, Reddit, and Hacker News every few weeks. It starts the same way:

> "5 years of LeetCode with no progress. I'm giving up."

The poster describes years of grinding. Hundreds of problems solved. Mock interviews. Premium subscriptions to **technical interview preparation** platforms. Courses. YouTube tutorials. And still—rejection after rejection.

What makes this post different from typical venting? The comments. Hundreds of engineers sharing the same story. Senior developers at Fortune 500 companies admitting they'd fail their own company's interview today.

**This isn't about individual failure. It's about a broken system that's burning out an entire profession.**

If you're feeling **coding interview anxiety**, you are not alone. This article explores why this is happening, the hidden costs of the current hiring landscape, and how to protect your mental health while navigating it.

---

## The Dirty Secret of Technical Interviews

Here's something interviewers won't tell you: your performance in a 45-minute whiteboard session has almost nothing to do with your ability to build software.

One senior engineer put it bluntly:

> "How you perform in an interview is not a measure of your worth as a software engineer—it is a measure of your ability to do interviews. The two are related but not equal."

Think about that. Years of building products, shipping features, mentoring teammates, debugging production systems at 2 AM—none of it matters when you're standing at a whiteboard trying to remember the optimal solution to "Trapping Rain Water."

The engineers who pass these **FAANG interviews** aren't necessarily better engineers. They're better at a very specific skill: performing algorithms under artificial pressure.

## Signs You're Experiencing LeetCode Burnout

**Tech interview burnout** is real, and it manifests in specific ways. It's not just "being tired." It's a fundamental depletion of your professional spirit.

### 1. Creative Death
One developer described it perfectly:
> "I wasn't struggling because I couldn't solve problems. I was struggling because I started dreading the whole ritual. My creativity felt drained. I stopped tinkering on side projects. Motivation dipped. I stopped coding for love and started coding just to pass a test."

### 2. Imposter Syndrome Amplification
You're a senior engineer at a major company. You build systems serving millions of users. But you can't solve a medium-difficulty graph problem in 25 minutes. What does that make you? A fraud, apparently. This disconnect fuels massive **imposter syndrome**.

### 3. Relationship Strain
Evenings spent grinding instead of with family. Weekends lost to practice problems. The guilt of choosing algorithm study over your kid's soccer game. The **opportunity cost** of interview prep is measured in life moments missed.

### 4. Career Doubt
After enough rejections, talented engineers start questioning everything:
- "Maybe I'm not cut out for this."
- "Maybe I should have become an accountant."
- "Maybe everyone else is smarter than me."

## The System Design Trap: Why Seniority Makes It Harder

If algorithm grinding is purgatory, **system design interviews** are hell—especially for senior engineers.

Here's the cruel irony: the more senior you become, the harder interviews get, but your daily work prepares you less for the specific format of the interview.

> "The reality is most developers don't get to design such systems in their daily jobs. And even if you're lucky to work at a company that runs large-scale, globally distributed systems, learning how they work on your own is not easy."

You're expected to design Twitter's architecture in 45 minutes. But at your actual job? You work on a specific service within a specific team. You've never seen the full architecture diagram. You've never made decisions at the Principal Engineer level.

The scope mismatch is absurd. You are being tested on the job of a CTO, but applying for the job of a Senior Engineer.

## The Physiology of Interview Anxiety

Let's talk about what nobody admits in public: the panic attacks.

> "Many REALLY good engineers have some form of anxiety disorder, and the standard practice for an interview is to put them in a pressure cooker situation."

When you are staring at a blank editor with a timer ticking down and a stranger watching your every keystroke, your body enters **fight or flight** mode. Cortisol floods your system. Your prefrontal cortex—the part of your brain responsible for complex logic and problem-solving—literally shuts down.

This isn't weakness. This is biology. We have designed a system that evaluates human worth through artificial stress tests that trigger our survival instincts.

## How to Cope: Surviving the Grind

If you are currently in the trenches of **coding interview prep**, here are some strategies to protect your sanity.

### 1. Change Your Metric of Success
Stop measuring your day by "problems solved." Measure it by "concepts understood." If you spent 3 hours on one problem and didn't solve it, but you learned deeply about how Hash Maps work, that is a win.

### 2. Simulate Reality, Not Just Code
One of the biggest sources of anxiety is the *environment*, not the *code*. Grinding LeetCode in silence doesn't prepare you for explaining your thought process to a human.
*   Practice talking out loud while you code.
*   Use tools that simulate the interview environment.
*   **Try our [Interview Simulator](/dashboard)**: We built this specifically to bridge the gap. It provides an AI coach that gives you real-time feedback, not just on your code, but on your *delivery* and *behavioral* answers. It helps desensitize you to the pressure.

### 3. The "Two-Fail" Rule
Give yourself permission to fail. If you fail an interview, allow yourself two days to feel bad, eat ice cream, and vent. Then, force a reset. Do not let one rejection spiral into a month of depression.

### 4. Target " sane" Companies
Not every company interviews like Google. There is a growing movement of companies that:
*   Give take-home assignments.
*   Pay you for a trial day.
*   Ask you to debug existing code instead of writing from scratch.
*   **Hiring Without Whiteboards** is a great resource to find these companies.

## The Unspoken Truth

Here's what experienced engineers know but rarely say out loud:

**The best job you'll ever have probably won't come from acing a LeetCode-style interview.**

It will come from:
- A referral from someone who's worked with you.
- A company that evaluates based on real work.
- A conversation where you discuss actual problems you've solved.
- A hiring manager who values demonstrated experience over puzzle performance.

The engineers who build the best careers aren't necessarily the best at interviews. They're the ones who find ways to demonstrate their value outside the traditional process.
