### Thread 5: "The Counterintuitive Way to Prepare for Behavioral Questions"

**Hook Tweet:**
Stop memorizing STAR stories. Everyone memorizes STAR stories. The engineers who stood out approached behavioral prep differently—and got faster, more authentic results. [BLOG_URL]

**Follow-up 1:**
The standard approach fails because:
1. You sound rehearsed (interviewers can tell)
2. Stories don't map cleanly to questions (you shoehorn answers)
3. You miss the actual question (searching for the "right" story while not listening)

The interviewer asking "tell me about a conflict" isn't checking your story bank. They're asking "can you navigate disagreement without burning relationships?"

**Follow-up 2:**
Better approach: prepare *understanding*, not answers. Do a career audit. Write down every project, challenge, relationship, decision, failure, success. Don't format it. Just dump it raw.

**Follow-up 3:**
Now find the *themes*. Maybe you repeatedly advocate for technical excellence. Or bridge communication gaps. Or fail when you don't speak up early. These themes are more valuable than any individual story because they're who you are.

**Follow-up 4:**
In the interview, construct stories real-time from your themes. "Tell me about a time you pushed back—" and you're drawing from your theme "I advocate for quality against deadline pressure" and finding a specific moment.

**Follow-up 5 (CTA):**
This approach requires building narrative fluency, not memorization. And that requires practice under realistic conditions—with follow-ups, with pressure, with someone probing your answers.

You finish an interview. The recruiter says, "You'll hear back in a week."

### Post 2: Consistency vs. Intensity

The problem with interview prep isn't that people don't study. It's that they study wrong.

The common approach: take two weeks off. Grind 8 hours a day. Marathon LeetCode sessions until you can't think straight.

By day 10, you're burned out. Anxious. Starting to hate coding.

I know because I was that person. Then I switched to something counterintuitive: 30 minutes every morning for three months.

Same total study time. Completely different results.

Here's why it works:

**Spaced repetition beats massed practice.** Your brain forms memories through repeated exposure over time, not marathon sessions. Solving one problem daily for 90 days beats 90 problems in one week.

**Sustainable beats heroic.** Most people can't maintain 4-hour daily study sessions while working full-time. 30 minutes is small enough that you'll actually do it. Every day. Even when you're tired.

**Decision fatigue is real.** When your plan is "do LeetCode," you waste mental energy deciding *what* to practice. A fixed routine eliminates decisions. You just execute.

The results timeline:
- Week 1-2: Feels slow. Patterns aren't clicking yet.
- Week 3-4: Patterns start clicking. You recognize problem types faster.
- Week 5-8: Speed improves dramatically. You're solving medium problems in the time limit.
- Month 4: Interview-ready. Patterns are automatic. You can focus on communication instead of struggling with code.

The meta-skill nobody talks about: this routine trains discipline, not just algorithms.

The engineers who succeed at FAANG don't have superhuman intelligence. They have superhuman consistency. They show up every day. No matter what.

Start tomorrow. 30 minutes. Same time, same place. For 90 days.

### Post 3: The Communication Gap

Here's what nobody tells you about technical interviews: most engineers prepare for the wrong interview.

They practice solving problems. They forget to practice *explaining* them.

So they walk into Google knowing the answer but sounding like a GPS having an existential crisis. Meanwhile, the candidate who got the offer explained the same solution in two sentences and sounded like they knew what they were doing.

The uncomfortable truth: you can know the right answer and still fail on communication.

I recorded myself during mock interviews. I sounded like I was translating from another language in my head. Because I basically was—translating my thoughts into words while also trying to think.

The fix isn't more practice problems. It's practicing the part of interviews you've been skipping: *speaking*.

Here's the framework that works: Hook-Approach-Walk-Through.

Hook (5 seconds): State your high-level strategy. "This is a two-pointer problem."

Approach (15-30 seconds): Explain your solution conceptually. "I'll maintain a pointer for each array and compare elements to find the smaller one."

Walk-Through (while coding): Narrate key decisions, not every keystroke. "I'm initializing the pointers... now the main loop..."

What's NOT in there: constant filler words, explaining obvious syntax, narrating your debugging in real-time.

The challenge is that communication is hard to practice alone. You need feedback. Not just "your code is correct." Feedback on clarity, pacing, whether you sound confident.

That's the gap between engineers who know their stuff and engineers who get offers.
